Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state or response lags behind the input, often forming a loop or memory effect.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt. When these materials are exposed to a magnetic field, they align their magnetic domains, and when the field is removed, the domains remain aligned to some extent, leading to a hysteresis loop.
Some Ceramics and Polymers: Certain types of ceramics and polymers can also show hysteresis under specific conditions, though it is less common than in metals.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Non-magnetic Metals: Pure metals like copper, aluminum, and gold do not exhibit significant hysteresis because they do not have magnetic domains that can align and realign.
Semiconductors and Insulators: These materials generally do not show hysteresis unless they are subjected to specific conditions that induce magnetic behavior (e.g., through doping or external fields).

Air:
Air itself does not have hysteresis. It is a non-magnetic, non-polarizable medium under normal conditions. However, if you consider the behavior of air in a system where it is compressed and expanded cyclically, it might exhibit some form of hysteresis due to changes in temperature and pressure, but this is not the same as magnetic hysteresis. For example, the volume of air in a gas cylinder might show some hysteresis-like behavior due to changes in temperature and pressure over multiple cycles, but this is not a true hysteresis loop in the same sense as seen in magnetic materials.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily observed in magnetic materials and some specific types of ceramics and polymers, while most other elements and materials do not exhibit this property.
