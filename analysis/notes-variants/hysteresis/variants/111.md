Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials or systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, rather than just the current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt. The most common example is ferrite, which is used in many electronic components.
Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel titanium alloy) can undergo shape changes based on temperature and can retain these changes, demonstrating hysteresis.
Piezoelectric Materials: Some piezoelectric materials show hysteresis in their response to mechanical stress and electrical fields.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Most non-magnetic materials do not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions. For example, copper, aluminum, and other metals that are not ferromagnetic do not show hysteresis.
Pure gases, including air, do not exhibit hysteresis because they do not have a memory effect related to past states.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
No, air does not have hysteresis. Air is a gas, and gases generally do not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions. The properties of air (such as pressure, volume, and temperature) change in a reversible manner, meaning that the system returns to its original state once the external conditions return to their original values. There is no "memory" effect in the behavior of air that would indicate hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily observed in specific types of materials, particularly those with magnetic properties or shape memory alloys. Air and other gases do not exhibit hysteresis due to their reversible and non-memory nature.
