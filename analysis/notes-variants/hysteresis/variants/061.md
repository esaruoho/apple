Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even when those conditions no longer exist.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material (like iron) is exposed to a magnetic field, it can retain some magnetization even after the external field is removed. This is known as remanence.
Rubber and Springs: These materials exhibit hysteresis in their stress-strain curves, meaning the energy required to deform them is not fully recovered when they return to their original shape.
Thermocouples: Some thermocouples show hysteresis in their temperature response due to changes in internal resistance or other factors.
Plastic Deformation: In materials that undergo plastic deformation, the stress-strain curve shows a hysteresis loop because the material does not fully return to its original state after being deformed.

Elements and Simple Systems:
Simple elements like gases, liquids, and pure metals typically do not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions. However, complex systems involving multiple components or interactions can exhibit hysteresis.

Things That Do Not Have Hysteresis:
Pure gases at constant temperature and pressure.
Pure liquids at constant temperature.
Pure metals under small deformations.
Simple electronic components like resistors and capacitors (under normal operating conditions).

Air:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions. It is a simple gas and does not retain any memory of past states in the way that magnetic materials or rubber do. However, if you consider more complex systems involving air, such as air conditioning systems or weather patterns, these can exhibit hysteresis-like behavior due to feedback mechanisms and time delays.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems, particularly those with memory effects or complex interactions. Simple elements like air do not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions.
