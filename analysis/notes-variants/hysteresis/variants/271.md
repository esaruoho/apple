Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state or response lags behind the changes in the input. Common examples of hysteresis include:

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Ferromagnetic materials like iron, nickel, and cobalt exhibit hysteresis in their magnetic properties. When these materials are exposed to a magnetic field, they retain some magnetization even after the field is removed.
Elastic Materials: Some elastic materials can show hysteresis in their stress-strain curves, particularly under cyclic loading conditions.
Thermal Systems: Certain thermal systems can exhibit hysteresis in their temperature responses, especially when there are phase transitions involved (e.g., melting and freezing).

Elements without Hysteresis:
Linear Systems: Most linear systems do not exhibit hysteresis. For example, resistors, capacitors, and inductors in electrical circuits behave linearly and do not remember past states.
Ideal Springs: Ideal springs follow Hooke's law and do not exhibit hysteresis.
Ohmic Conductors: Conductors with constant resistance do not show hysteresis.

Air and Hysteresis:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis because it is an ideal gas under normal conditions. It does not retain any memory of past pressure or temperature changes. However, certain properties related to air can exhibit hysteresis under specific conditions:

Humidity in Air: The moisture content in air can exhibit some form of hysteresis, particularly in the context of desiccation and hydration cycles. For instance, the equilibrium relative humidity of a material can depend on its previous exposure to different humidity levels.

In summary, while many materials and systems can exhibit hysteresis, air itself does not typically show this behavior under normal conditions.
