Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, particularly ferromagnetic materials like iron, nickel, and cobalt. When these materials are exposed to a magnetic field, they align their magnetic domains, and when the field is removed, the domains remain aligned to some extent, leading to a hysteresis loop.
Some Ceramics and Polymers: Certain types of ceramics and polymers can also show hysteresis under specific conditions, such as mechanical deformation or electrical polarization.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Most non-magnetic materials do not exhibit hysteresis. For example, metals like copper, aluminum, and gold do not show significant hysteresis unless they are subjected to special treatments or conditions.
Pure gases, including air, do not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
Air, being a gas, does not have hysteresis under normal conditions. Gases do not retain memory of previous states in the way that magnetic materials do. If you change the pressure or temperature of air, it will respond immediately and directly to the new conditions without any lag or dependence on past conditions.

However, if you were to consider more complex systems involving air, such as a gas-filled spring or a gas-damping mechanism, there might be some form of hysteresis due to the mechanical properties of the system rather than the intrinsic properties of the air itself.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily associated with materials that can store and remember magnetic or other forms of energy, while simple gases like air do not exhibit this property under normal circumstances.
