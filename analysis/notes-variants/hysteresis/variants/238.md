What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed. Hysteresis is commonly seen in magnetic materials, rubber, and some types of mechanical systems.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetized material (like iron) is exposed to a magnetic field, it retains some magnetization even after the field is removed. The relationship between the magnetic field strength and the magnetization is hysteresis.
Rubber: Rubber exhibits hysteresis in its stress-strain curve. When stretched, it requires more energy than when it is being relaxed, due to internal friction within the polymer chains.
Mechanical Systems: Some mechanical systems, like springs with friction or dampers, can exhibit hysteresis in their force-displacement curves.

Elements and Materials Without Hysteresis:
Many elements and simple materials do not exhibit hysteresis. For example:
Metals: Pure metals without magnetic properties do not show hysteresis.
Non-magnetic Materials: Materials that do not become magnetized, such as copper or aluminum, do not show hysteresis.
Simple Mechanical Systems: Ideal springs without friction do not exhibit hysteresis.

Air:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis. It is a simple gas and does not retain any memory of past conditions. However, certain properties of air, such as its pressure and temperature, can be described using thermodynamic cycles, which might appear similar to hysteresis in some contexts. But these are not true cases of hysteresis because they do not depend on the history of the system.

In summary, hysteresis is a property of specific materials and systems, particularly those involving magnetic fields, internal friction, or complex mechanical interactions. Air, being a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis.
