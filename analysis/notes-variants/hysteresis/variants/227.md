Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time depends on the path it has taken to get there, not just its current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis

Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, particularly ferromagnetic materials like iron, nickel, and cobalt. The magnetization of these materials depends on their past exposure to magnetic fields.
Some Ferroelectric Materials: These materials can also show hysteresis in their polarization properties when subjected to electric fields.
Shape Memory Alloys: These alloys change shape in response to temperature changes and can exhibit hysteresis in their phase transitions.
Piezoelectric Materials: Some piezoelectric materials can show hysteresis in their electrical responses to mechanical stress.

Elements without Hysteresis

Most simple elements or pure substances do not exhibit hysteresis because they do not have complex internal structures or memory effects. For example:
Metals (like copper, gold)
Non-magnetic insulators
Pure gases (like nitrogen, oxygen)

Does Air Have Hysteresis?

Air, as a gas, does not typically exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions. Gases generally follow simple thermodynamic laws where the state variables (such as pressure, volume, and temperature) depend only on the current conditions, not on the history of those conditions.

However, in very specific contexts or under extreme conditions, some unusual behaviors might be observed, but these would not be considered true hysteresis in the same way as seen in magnetic materials or other complex systems.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily observed in complex materials and systems, especially those with internal memory or phase transitions. Simple elements and gases like air do not exhibit hysteresis under normal circumstances.
