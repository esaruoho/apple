What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, rather than just the current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis:

Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, particularly ferromagnetic materials like iron, nickel, and cobalt. When these materials are exposed to a magnetic field, they can retain some magnetization even after the field is removed. The relationship between the magnetic field strength (H) and the resulting magnetization (M) is described by the hysteresis loop.

Some Ferroelectric Materials: Certain ferroelectric materials also show hysteresis in their polarization behavior when subjected to electric fields.

Shape Memory Alloys: These alloys can change shape in response to temperature changes and exhibit hysteresis in their transformation behavior.

Piezoelectric Materials: Some piezoelectric materials show hysteresis in their electrical response to mechanical stress.

Elements without Hysteresis:

Non-Ferromagnetic Metals: Materials like copper, aluminum, and gold do not exhibit significant hysteresis because they do not retain magnetic properties after the removal of an external magnetic field.

Semiconductors: While semiconductors can exhibit some form of hysteresis in their electrical properties under certain conditions, it is generally much less pronounced than in magnetic or ferroelectric materials.

Insulators: Pure insulating materials typically do not show hysteresis unless they are subjected to specific conditions that induce a reversible phase change.

Air:

Air itself does not have hysteresis. It is a gas and does not retain any memory of past states in the way that magnetic materials do. However, air can exhibit some hysteretic behavior in certain contexts, such as in the case of humidity. For example, the amount of water vapor that air can hold at a given temperature can depend on whether the air was previously saturated or not, but this is not the same as the hysteresis seen in magnetic materials.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily observed in materials and systems that can store and release energy in a reversible manner, such as magnetic and ferroelectric materials, shape memory alloys, and some piezoelectric materials. Air, being a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis in the same sense.
