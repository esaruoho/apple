Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, rather than just the current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt. When these materials are exposed to a magnetic field, they retain some magnetization even after the field is removed. The relationship between the magnetic field strength (H) and the resulting magnetization (M) is described by the hysteresis loop.
Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel titanium alloy) can change shape in response to temperature changes, and this behavior can also show hysteresis.
Some Polymers: Certain polymers can exhibit hysteresis in their mechanical properties under cyclic loading.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Non-Ferromagnetic Metals: Materials like copper, aluminum, and gold do not show significant hysteresis because they do not retain magnetization after the magnetic field is removed.
Semiconductors: While semiconductors can exhibit some form of hysteresis in certain contexts (e.g., in memory devices), they generally do not show the same type of magnetic hysteresis as ferromagnetic materials.
Insulators: Most insulating materials do not show hysteresis unless they are subjected to specific conditions that induce it.

Air:
Air itself does not have hysteresis. It is a simple gas and does not retain any state information from previous conditions. However, air can be involved in processes that might exhibit hysteresis, such as the behavior of air in a compressor or an expansion valve in a refrigeration cycle, but the air itself does not have intrinsic hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is a property primarily associated with certain materials and systems, particularly those involving magnetic fields or specific types of mechanical deformation. Air, being a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis.
