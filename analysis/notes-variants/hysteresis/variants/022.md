Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials or systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, rather than just the current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis

Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, including iron, nickel, and cobalt. The most common example is ferrite, which is used in various electronic components.
Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel titanium alloy) can undergo shape changes based on temperature and can remember their original shape after being deformed.
Some Ceramics: Certain types of ceramics can show hysteresis in their electrical properties.

Elements without Hysteresis

Most simple substances do not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions. For example:
Water: While water can exhibit some unusual behavior (like the anomalous expansion of ice), it does not show hysteresis in its basic phase transitions.
Gases: Pure gases typically do not show hysteresis in their phase transitions.
Solids: Most solid materials do not show hysteresis unless they are specifically engineered to do so.

Air and Hysteresis

Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis because it is a simple gas and does not have memory-like properties. However, the behavior of air in certain systems (like in humidity control systems) might be modeled using hysteretic behavior due to the complex interactions between temperature and humidity. But this is an approximation and not inherent to the air itself.

In summary, hysteresis is a property primarily seen in specific materials and systems, particularly those involving magnetic fields, shape memory alloys, and some specialized ceramics. Air, as a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis.
