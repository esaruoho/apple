Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials or systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, rather than just the current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis

Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, including iron, nickel, and cobalt. The most common example is ferrite, which is used in various electronic components due to its magnetic properties.
Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel titanium alloy) can change shape based on temperature changes, and they exhibit hysteresis in their phase transitions.
Some Ceramics: Certain types of ceramics can show hysteresis in their electrical properties under varying conditions.

Elements without Hysteresis

Most simple elements do not exhibit hysteresis because they do not have complex internal structures or memory effects. For example:

Metals: Pure metals generally do not show significant hysteresis unless they are part of a composite material or undergo phase changes.
Non-magnetic Metals: Metals like copper, aluminum, and gold do not show magnetic hysteresis because they do not retain a permanent magnetic field.

Air and Hysteresis

Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis because it is a simple gas and does not have the complex internal structure or memory effects required for hysteresis. However, certain properties of air can be influenced by past conditions, such as humidity or pressure changes, but these are not examples of hysteresis in the strict sense.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily observed in materials with complex internal structures or memory effects, such as magnetic materials and shape memory alloys. Simple elements and gases like air do not exhibit hysteresis.
