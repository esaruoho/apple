What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time is influenced by past states as well. Here are some examples of elements and systems that exhibit hysteresis:

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material is exposed to a magnetic field, it can retain some magnetization even after the field is removed. This is known as remanence. The relationship between the magnetic field strength (H) and the magnetic flux density (B) is not linear but forms a loop, which is the hysteresis loop.
Solenoids: In a solenoid, the magnetic field strength depends on the number of turns and the current, but it also retains some residual magnetism, showing hysteresis.
Rubber Bands: When stretched, rubber bands exhibit hysteresis in their stress-strain curve. The force required to stretch the band depends on how much it was previously stretched.
Thermocouples: Some thermocouples show hysteresis in their temperature response due to changes in internal resistance or other factors.

Examples of Non-Hysteresis:
Linear Springs: Springs that follow Hooke's Law (F = kx, where F is the force, k is the spring constant, and x is the displacement) do not exhibit hysteresis because the force is directly proportional to the displacement without any memory of past displacements.
Ohmic Conductors: In an ohmic conductor, the current is directly proportional to the voltage (I = V/R), and there is no hysteresis because the relationship is linear and does not depend on past conditions.
Ideal Capacitors and Inductors: These components do not exhibit hysteresis because they store energy in a way that is independent of past conditions. For example, the charge on a capacitor or the magnetic flux in an inductor depends only on the current or voltage at the moment, not on previous values.

Air:
Air does not exhibit hysteresis in its basic properties. The pressure and volume relationship described by Boyle's Law (P ∝ 1/V for a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature) or the ideal gas law (PV = nRT) is linear and does not show hysteresis. However, if you consider more complex phenomena involving air, such as phase transitions (e.g., condensation and evaporation of water vapor), these processes might show some form of hysteresis due to the history of the phase changes. But this is not a fundamental property of air itself.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems, particularly those with memory effects, while others follow simple, linear relationships without retaining past states.
