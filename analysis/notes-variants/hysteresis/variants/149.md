What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time depends on its past states as well.

Examples of Hysteresis:

Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material is exposed to a magnetic field, it can retain some magnetization even after the field is removed. This is known as remanence. The relationship between the magnetic field strength (H) and the magnetic flux density (B) is not linear; it forms a loop called the hysteresis loop.
Elastic Deformation: Some materials exhibit elastic hysteresis, meaning that the stress-strain curve does not return to zero when the applied force is removed. Instead, there is a residual deformation.
Thermal Systems: In some thermal systems, the temperature response can depend on the previous temperature changes, especially in systems with memory effects like phase change materials.
Mechanical Springs: In some mechanical systems, the force required to stretch or compress a spring can depend on how much it was previously stretched or compressed.

Elements That Do Not Have Hysteresis:

Linear Systems: These systems respond in a way that is directly proportional to the input. There is no memory effect, and the output does not depend on the history of inputs.
Ideal Capacitors and Inductors: In ideal electrical components, the voltage across a capacitor or the current through an inductor is directly proportional to the charge or magnetic flux, respectively, without any hysteresis.

Air and Hysteresis:

Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis because it is a simple gas and does not have the complex internal structure or memory effects that materials like magnetic materials do. However, properties related to air can show hysteresis under certain conditions. For example:

Humidity in Air: The amount of water vapor in the air can depend on the previous humidity levels, especially in closed systems. This can be somewhat hysteresis-like behavior, but it is not a standard form of hysteresis seen in materials.
Pressure and Temperature: In thermodynamic cycles involving air, the pressure and temperature can trace out a loop similar to a hysteresis loop, but this is more about the cycle of processes rather than a direct hysteresis effect.

In summary, hysteresis is a property of specific materials and systems, and air itself does not exhibit hysteresis in the same sense as magnetic materials or other complex systems.
