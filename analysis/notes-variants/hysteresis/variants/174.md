Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time depends on its past states as well.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt. When these materials are exposed to a magnetic field, they retain some magnetization even after the field is removed, leading to a hysteresis loop.
Shape Memory Alloys (SMAs): These alloys, like nitinol, can change shape in response to temperature changes and retain this shape until the temperature is changed again. The process of heating and cooling can create a hysteresis effect.
Piezoelectric Materials: Some piezoelectric materials show hysteresis in their electrical response to mechanical stress.
Thermocouples and Thermistors: These devices can exhibit hysteresis in their resistance-temperature relationship under certain conditions.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Most non-magnetic materials do not exhibit hysteresis. For example, metals like copper or aluminum, which are not ferromagnetic, do not show hysteresis in their magnetic properties. Similarly, most gases and liquids do not exhibit hysteresis in their physical properties under normal conditions.

Air:
Air, being a gas, does not exhibit hysteresis in its typical physical properties such as pressure, volume, and temperature. However, if you consider more complex systems involving air, such as in the context of phase transitions or in certain types of sensors, there might be some behavior that could be described as hysteresis, but it would be very specific and not a general property of air itself.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily associated with materials and systems that can store and release energy in a way that depends on their past states, such as magnetic materials and shape memory alloys. Air, as a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis in its basic physical properties.
