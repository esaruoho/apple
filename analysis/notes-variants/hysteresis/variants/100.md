What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed. Materials with hysteresis typically exhibit behaviors such as magnetic fields, stress-strain relationships in ferromagnetic materials, and phase transitions.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetized material is exposed to an external magnetic field, its magnetization will lag behind the change in the external field. This is known as the hysteresis loop.
Phase Transitions: In some materials, the transition between phases (e.g., solid to liquid) can be hysteresis-dependent, meaning the reverse transition occurs at a different temperature or pressure.
Elasticity: Some elastic materials show hysteresis in their stress-strain curves, particularly if they undergo cyclic loading.

Elements and Simple Systems:
Air: Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis because it is a simple gas without internal memory or complex interactions that would cause such behavior. The properties of air (such as density, pressure, and temperature) do not depend on past conditions in a hysteresis manner.

Non-Hysteresis Examples:
Linear Systems: Most linear systems, like resistors or capacitors, do not exhibit hysteresis. Their behavior is directly proportional to the input.
Simple Mechanical Systems: Basic mechanical components like springs (when not subjected to large deformations) or dampers typically do not show hysteresis unless they are part of a more complex system.

In summary, air does not have hysteresis because it is a simple, non-memory material. Hysteresis is more commonly associated with complex materials and systems that have internal states that "remember" past conditions.
