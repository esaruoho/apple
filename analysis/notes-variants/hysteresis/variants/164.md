What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, rather than just the current conditions.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material is exposed to an external magnetic field, it can retain some magnetization even after the external field is removed. This is known as remanence.
Rubber and Springs: These materials exhibit hysteresis due to their elastic properties. The stress-strain relationship is not linear, and the material will deform more for a given stress during loading compared to unloading.
Thermocouples: Some thermocouples show hysteresis due to changes in their electrical resistance with temperature.
Solenoids and Relays: The switching behavior of these devices can be hysteresis-dependent.

Elements and Substances That Do Not Typically Have Hysteresis:
Most Metals and Pure Elements: Pure metals and simple elements do not typically exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions.
Air: Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis. It is a simple gas whose properties (like pressure and volume) follow straightforward relationships described by the ideal gas law, without memory of past states.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
No, air does not have hysteresis. The state of air (its pressure, temperature, and volume) follows well-defined equations of state, such as the ideal gas law, which do not depend on the history of the system. Changes in air properties are reversible and do not "remember" previous states.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of specific materials and systems, particularly those with complex internal structures or nonlinear responses. Simple substances like air do not exhibit hysteresis.
