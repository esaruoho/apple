What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials or systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time depends on the path it took to get there, not just its current conditions.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material is exposed to a magnetic field, it can retain some magnetization even after the external field is removed. This is known as remanence and is a classic example of hysteresis.
Rubber and Springs: The deformation of rubber or other elastic materials under stress can exhibit hysteresis, meaning the amount of deformation depends on the history of stress applied.
Thermocouples: In some cases, thermocouples can show hysteresis, especially if they are subjected to rapid temperature changes.
Soil and Water Content: In soil mechanics, the relationship between water content and soil strength can exhibit hysteresis.

Elements and Simple Systems:
Elements and simple systems generally do not exhibit hysteresis because they do not have complex internal states or memory effects. For example, a single element like copper will not show hysteresis in its electrical resistance when a magnetic field is applied and then removed.

Things That Do Not Have Hysteresis:
Most simple mechanical systems (like a rigid rod)
Pure fluids (liquids and gases) under normal conditions
Pure metals without magnetic properties

Air:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis. It is a simple gas and does not have the complex internal structure or memory effects required for hysteresis. However, in certain contexts, such as phase transitions or complex fluid dynamics, air can behave in ways that might seem similar to hysteresis, but these are usually due to other physical phenomena rather than true hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is a property seen in specific materials and systems with complex internal states or memory effects, while simple elements and systems generally do not exhibit this behavior.
