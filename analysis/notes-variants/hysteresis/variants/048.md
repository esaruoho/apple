Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials or systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, rather than just the current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, including iron, nickel, and cobalt. The most common example is ferrite, which is used in various electronic components.
Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel-titanium alloy) can undergo shape changes based on temperature, and their behavior can show hysteresis.
Some Ceramics: Certain types of ceramics can also exhibit hysteresis under specific conditions.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Most non-magnetic materials do not exhibit hysteresis. Examples include copper, aluminum, and most plastics.
Pure metals that do not form magnetic domains typically do not show hysteresis.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
Air itself does not have hysteresis because it is not a material that retains memory of past states in the way that magnetic materials do. However, certain properties of air can change in a way that might seem similar to hysteresis under specific conditions:

Thermal Properties: The thermal capacity of air can change depending on its previous heating or cooling cycles, but this is not true hysteresis. It’s more about the transient response of the system rather than a persistent memory effect.
Humidity: The amount of water vapor in the air can change based on previous humidity levels, but this is due to physical processes rather than a hysteresis loop.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily observed in materials that can store and retain information about their past states, such as magnetic materials and some alloys. Air, being a gas, does not exhibit hysteresis in the same sense.
