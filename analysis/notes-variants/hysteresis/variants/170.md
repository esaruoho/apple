What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, not just the current conditions.

Examples of Hysteresis:

Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, particularly ferromagnetic materials like iron, nickel, and cobalt. The magnetization (M) of these materials depends on the history of applied magnetic fields (H). This is why you need to apply a reverse field to demagnetize such materials fully.

Rubber and Polymers: Some rubber and polymer materials show hysteresis in their stress-strain curves. This means that the strain experienced by the material depends on the previous stresses it has undergone.

Saturable Inductors: In electrical circuits, saturable inductors can exhibit hysteresis in their inductance as a function of the current passing through them.

Thermocouples: Certain types of thermocouples can show hysteresis in their temperature response due to changes in the material properties with temperature.

Soil and Rocks: In geology, some soils and rocks can exhibit hysteresis in their stress-strain behavior under cyclic loading.

Elements and Pure Substances:

Pure elements or simple substances generally do not exhibit hysteresis because they do not have complex internal structures or phase transitions that depend on past conditions. For example, pure metals like copper or gold do not show significant hysteresis in their mechanical or magnetic properties.

Air:

Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis. It is a simple gas and does not have the complex internal structure or phase transitions necessary for hysteresis. However, if you consider the behavior of air in certain systems, such as in a compressor or an expansion valve in a refrigeration cycle, you might observe hysteresis-like effects due to the changing states of the air, but this is not inherent to the air itself.

In summary, hysteresis is a property of certain materials and systems, particularly those with complex internal structures or phase transitions. Simple elements and pure substances generally do not exhibit hysteresis, and air itself does not show this property.
