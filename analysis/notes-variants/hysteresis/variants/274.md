What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, not just the current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt. The magnetic field strength (H) versus magnetic flux density (B) curve forms a loop, known as the hysteresis loop.
Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel titanium alloy) can undergo shape changes based on temperature, and their behavior can show hysteresis.
Some Ceramics and Polymers: Certain types of ceramics and some polymers can also exhibit hysteresis under specific conditions.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Non-Ferromagnetic Metals: Materials like copper, aluminum, and gold do not show significant hysteresis because they do not retain a permanent magnetic field.
Semiconductors: While some semiconductors can show hysteresis in certain applications, many do not.
Insulators: Pure insulators generally do not show hysteresis unless they are subjected to specific conditions that induce it.

Air:
Air itself does not have hysteresis. It is a gas and does not retain any memory of past states in the way that materials with hysteresis do. For example, if you apply a magnetic field to a piece of iron and then remove it, the iron will retain some magnetization (until it reaches its coercive field), which is a form of hysteresis. However, air does not exhibit this behavior; it simply returns to its original state once the external influence is removed.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems, particularly those with memory effects like magnetic materials and shape memory alloys. Air, being a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis.
