Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time depends on the path it took to arrive there, not just its current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, particularly in their magnetic properties. When these materials are exposed to a magnetic field, they can retain some magnetization even after the external field is removed. This is why you see a hysteresis loop when plotting the magnetic field strength against the magnetization.
Rubber and Elastomers: These materials show hysteresis in their stress-strain curves, meaning the energy required to deform them depends on the previous deformation history.
Some Ceramics and Polymers: Certain types of ceramics and polymers can also exhibit hysteresis in their mechanical or electrical properties.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Linear Systems: In linear systems, the response to an input is directly proportional to the input itself, and does not depend on past inputs. Examples include simple resistors, capacitors, and inductors in electrical circuits.
Ideal Springs and Masses: Idealized models of springs and masses do not exhibit hysteresis; they return to their original state without retaining any residual deformation or energy.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
Air, as a gas, does not exhibit hysteresis in its typical physical properties such as pressure, volume, and temperature under normal conditions. The behavior of gases is generally described by the ideal gas law, which is a linear relationship between pressure, volume, and temperature. However, under extreme conditions (e.g., phase transitions, non-ideal behavior), gases might show some deviations from ideal behavior, but these are not typically described using the concept of hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of specific materials and systems, particularly those with memory effects like magnetic materials and certain elastomers. Air, under normal conditions, does not exhibit hysteresis.
