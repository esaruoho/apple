What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed. Hysteresis is commonly seen in magnetic materials, rubber, and some mechanical systems.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: The magnetization of ferromagnetic materials like iron depends on the history of applied magnetic fields. This is why a magnet can retain its magnetism even after the external magnetic field is removed.
Rubber: When stretched, rubber exhibits hysteresis in its stress-strain curve. The material requires more energy to stretch further, and it takes less energy to return to its original shape, leading to a loop in the stress-strain graph.
Thermal Systems: Some phase transitions, such as the melting and freezing of water, can exhibit hysteresis, especially if the process is conducted slowly.

Elements and Simple Substances:
Simple elements or pure substances typically do not exhibit hysteresis because they do not have complex internal structures or interactions that would lead to memory effects. For example, pure metals like copper or silver do not show significant hysteresis in their properties.

Air:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis. It is a simple gas with no internal structure that would remember past conditions. However, certain properties of air, such as its density or viscosity, might change depending on temperature and pressure, but these changes do not form a hysteresis loop unless specific conditions are met (e.g., in certain phase transitions or complex systems involving air).

In summary, hysteresis is a property of certain materials and systems, particularly those with complex internal structures or phase transitions. Simple elements and gases like air do not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions.
