Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time is influenced by its past states.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, particularly when they are subjected to repeated cycles of magnetization and demagnetization. Examples include iron, nickel, and cobalt.
Shape Memory Alloys: These alloys can change shape in response to temperature changes and retain this shape until the temperature is changed again. They show hysteresis in their stress-strain curves.
Piezoelectric Materials: Some piezoelectric materials exhibit hysteresis in their electrical response to mechanical stress.
Some Polymers: Certain polymers can show hysteresis in their mechanical properties under cyclic loading.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Most non-magnetic materials do not exhibit hysteresis. For example, copper, aluminum, and most plastics do not show significant hysteresis.
Linear systems, which respond in a direct and proportional manner to their inputs, do not exhibit hysteresis.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
Air itself does not have hysteresis because it is a simple gas and does not retain memory of previous states in the way that materials like magnetic materials do. However, certain properties of air, such as its pressure or temperature, can be described using hysteresis-like behavior in specific contexts (e.g., in cyclic processes involving compression and expansion). But air itself as a substance does not inherently possess hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems, particularly those that can store and recall information about past states. Air, being a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis in the same sense.
