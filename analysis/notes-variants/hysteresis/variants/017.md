What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed. Hysteresis loops are often seen in magnetic materials, rubber, and some types of mechanical systems.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetized material (like iron) is exposed to a magnetic field, it retains some magnetization even after the external field is removed. This is known as remanence.
Rubber: Rubber exhibits hysteresis in its stress-strain curve, meaning the energy required to deform the rubber depends on the previous deformation history.
Solenoids: In electrical circuits, solenoids can exhibit hysteresis in their magnetic properties when used as switches or relays.

Elements and Other Non-Hysteresis Examples:
Metals like Copper and Aluminum: These do not show significant hysteresis in their electrical resistance under normal operating conditions.
Non-magnetic Materials: Materials that do not become magnetized, such as wood or plastic, do not exhibit hysteresis.
Pure Gases: Pure gases generally do not show hysteresis unless they undergo phase transitions (e.g., liquid-gas transitions).

Air:
Air, being a gas, does not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions. It does not retain any memory of past states in terms of its physical properties like density or pressure. However, if you consider phase transitions (such as condensation and vaporization), these processes can be somewhat hysteresis-like, but this is more about the thermodynamic cycle rather than a continuous hysteresis loop.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of specific materials and systems, particularly those with memory effects related to their state history. Air, as a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis.
