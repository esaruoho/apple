Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even when those conditions no longer exist. Hysteresis is commonly seen in magnetic materials, rubber, and some mechanical systems.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetized material (like iron) is exposed to a magnetic field, it retains some of its magnetization even after the external field is removed. This is known as remanence.
Rubber: Rubber exhibits hysteresis in its stress-strain curve. The energy required to stretch the rubber is higher than the energy released during relaxation, leading to an elastic hysteresis loop.
Solenoids: In a solenoid, the current required to maintain a certain magnetic flux density can be different from the current required to achieve the same flux density when decreasing the current.

Elements That Do Not Have Hysteresis:
Non-magnetic Materials: Materials like copper, aluminum, and most non-magnetic metals do not exhibit hysteresis because they do not retain any magnetic properties after the removal of an external magnetic field.
Linear Systems: Systems that follow linear relationships between input and output do not exhibit hysteresis. For example, Ohm's law for resistors is a linear relationship and does not show hysteresis.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
Air, being a gas, does not exhibit hysteresis in the same way as magnetic materials or rubber. The state of air (pressure, temperature, volume) changes in response to immediate environmental conditions without retaining a memory of past states. Therefore, air does not have hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems, particularly those with memory effects such as magnetic materials and rubber. Air, being a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis.
