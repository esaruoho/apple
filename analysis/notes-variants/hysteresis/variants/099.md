What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material (like iron) is exposed to a magnetic field, it becomes magnetized. If the magnetic field is then removed, the material may retain some magnetization, depending on the strength and direction of the field. This is known as remanence.
Rubber Bands: When you stretch a rubber band, it will return to its original shape when released, but if you stretch it beyond a certain point, it might not fully return to its original length. The amount of stretch depends on how much it was stretched previously.
Solenoids: In electrical circuits, solenoids can exhibit hysteresis in their magnetic properties, similar to magnetic materials.
Thermal Systems: Some thermal systems can exhibit hysteresis, such as phase transitions in materials like water freezing and melting.

Elements and Hysteresis:
Elements: Pure elements do not typically exhibit hysteresis because they do not have complex internal structures or memory mechanisms. However, compounds or alloys made from these elements can show hysteresis due to their more complex interactions.

Things That Do Not Have Hysteresis:
Most simple mechanical systems (like a spring without any memory effects).
Pure gases (like air) under normal conditions do not exhibit hysteresis. They follow ideal gas laws and do not "remember" previous states.

Air and Hysteresis:
Air: Under normal conditions and typical pressures and temperatures, air does not exhibit hysteresis. It follows the ideal gas law, which describes the behavior of gases based on temperature and pressure without any memory of past conditions.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems that can "remember" past conditions, while simple elements and gases like air do not exhibit this property under normal conditions.
