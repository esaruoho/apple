Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed. Common examples of hysteresis include magnetic materials, rubber, and some types of springs.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: These materials exhibit hysteresis when they are exposed to varying magnetic fields. The magnetization (or magnetic moment) of these materials depends on both the current magnetic field and the history of previous magnetic fields.
Rubber and Other Elastomers: These materials can deform elastically, and their stress-strain relationship can show hysteresis, meaning the material will store energy during deformation and release it during unloading, leading to a loop in the stress-strain curve.
Some Types of Springs: Certain types of springs, especially those made from materials like steel, can exhibit hysteresis under cyclic loading.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Linear Materials: Materials that follow Hooke's Law (where stress is directly proportional to strain) do not exhibit hysteresis. Examples include many metals and some plastics under small deformations.
Ideal Fluids: Fluids that follow ideal behavior (like water or air under certain conditions) do not exhibit hysteresis because their properties do not depend on past states.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
Air, as an ideal gas under normal conditions, does not exhibit hysteresis. Its properties (such as pressure, volume, and temperature) are linearly related within the limits of the ideal gas law, and there is no memory effect that would cause the properties to depend on past conditions. However, under extreme conditions (e.g., very high pressures or temperatures), real gases may deviate from ideal behavior, but this deviation is not typically described as hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems, particularly those that are nonlinear or have memory effects. Air, as an ideal gas, does not exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions.
