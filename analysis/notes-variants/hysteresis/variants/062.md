Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state or response lags behind the input, often forming a loop or memory effect.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt. When these materials are exposed to a magnetic field, they align their magnetic domains, and when the field is removed, the domains do not immediately return to their original state but remain aligned for some time.
Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel-titanium alloy) can undergo shape changes based on temperature and can retain those shapes until the material is heated above a certain threshold, demonstrating hysteresis.
Some Polymers: Certain polymers can show hysteresis in their mechanical properties under stress and strain cycles.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Non-Ferromagnetic Metals: Materials like copper, aluminum, and gold do not exhibit significant hysteresis because their magnetic domains do not align in a way that retains a memory of past magnetic fields.
Semiconductors: While semiconductors can exhibit some form of hysteresis in certain conditions (like in ferroelectric materials), most common semiconductor materials do not show pronounced hysteresis effects.
Insulators: Most insulating materials do not show hysteresis unless they are specifically engineered to do so.

Air:
Air itself does not have hysteresis. It is a simple gas and does not retain any memory of past states in the way that materials with hysteresis do. For example, if you compress air and then release it, the air will simply expand back to its original volume without retaining any "memory" of the compression cycle.

In summary, hysteresis is a property seen in specific materials and systems, particularly those with magnetic or shape-memory characteristics. Air, being a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis.
