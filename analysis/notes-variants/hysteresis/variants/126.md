Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time depends on its past states as well. Here are some examples and non-examples of hysteresis:

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material (like iron) is exposed to a magnetic field, it can retain some magnetization even after the external field is removed. This is known as remanence. The magnetization (output) lags behind the applied magnetic field (input), creating a hysteresis loop.
Rubber Bands: When you stretch a rubber band, it will return to its original shape when you release it, but the amount of stretch required to reach the same point again is different due to the elastic properties of the material.
Solenoids: In a solenoid, the current required to achieve a certain magnetic flux density is dependent on the previous state of the solenoid. Reversing the direction of the current also requires more or less energy depending on the previous state.

Non-Examples of Hysteresis:
Linear Systems: Systems that follow linear relationships between inputs and outputs do not exhibit hysteresis. For example, a simple resistor in an electrical circuit follows Ohm's law (V = IR), where the voltage across the resistor is directly proportional to the current through it, without any memory of past states.
Ideal Springs: An ideal spring follows Hooke's law (F = kx), where the force exerted by the spring is directly proportional to the displacement from its equilibrium position. There is no lag or dependency on past states.

Air:
Air does not exhibit hysteresis in the same way as materials or systems with memory. However, there are some phenomena related to air that might seem similar to hysteresis:

Thermal Properties: While air itself doesn't show hysteresis, the thermal properties of air can exhibit behavior that resembles hysteresis in certain contexts. For example, the temperature of air in a closed system can change in a way that depends on the history of heating and cooling cycles, but this is more about the thermal capacity and heat transfer characteristics rather than intrinsic hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems where the response depends on the history of inputs. Air, being a fluid, does not inherently exhibit hysteresis, although its thermal properties can sometimes mimic similar behavior under certain conditions.
