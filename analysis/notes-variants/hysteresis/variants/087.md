What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials or systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, not just the current conditions.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material (like iron) is exposed to a magnetic field, it can retain some magnetization even after the field is removed. This is known as remanence and is a classic example of hysteresis.
Solenoids and Relays: These devices exhibit hysteresis because they require a certain threshold of current to switch states, and once switched, they remain in that state until another threshold is reached.
Rubber Bands: The deformation of rubber bands shows hysteresis because the force required to stretch them back to their original shape depends on how far they were stretched initially.
Thermocouples: In some cases, thermocouples can show hysteresis due to the thermal history of the material.

Elements and Simple Substances:
Simple elements like metals, gases, and pure liquids do not typically exhibit hysteresis under normal conditions. They respond linearly to changes in external parameters such as temperature or pressure.

Air:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis in the same way that materials do. However, properties related to air, such as its density or viscosity, can change depending on past conditions. For example, if air is compressed and then allowed to expand, the final state might depend on the history of compression and expansion cycles. But this is more about the behavior of the system rather than a hysteresis loop in the material itself.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily associated with specific types of materials and systems, while simple elements and substances generally do not exhibit this property.
