What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even when those conditions no longer exist.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: The magnetization of ferromagnetic materials like iron, nickel, and cobalt depends on the history of magnetic fields applied to them. This is why these materials can retain their magnetization after the external field is removed.
Rubber and Elastic Materials: These materials exhibit hysteresis in their stress-strain curves. When stretched and released, they do not return to their original shape immediately; instead, they follow a different path during compression compared to stretching.
Thermocouples: Some thermocouples exhibit hysteresis in their temperature-to-voltage response, meaning the voltage output may differ depending on whether the temperature is increasing or decreasing.
Solenoids and Relays: The switching behavior of these devices can show hysteresis, where the threshold for activation differs from the threshold for deactivation.

Elements and Simple Systems:
Simple elements like gases (e.g., air), pure metals, or basic electronic components typically do not exhibit significant hysteresis unless they are part of a more complex system or material.

Air:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis because it is a simple gas with no internal memory or state retention properties. The pressure and volume relationship described by Boyle's Law or the Ideal Gas Law is straightforward and does not depend on past conditions.

Summary:
Has Hysteresis: Magnetic materials, rubber, some thermocouples, solenoids, relays.
Does Not Have Hysteresis: Simple elements like air, pure metals, or basic electronic components.
Air: Does not have hysteresis.
