Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, including iron, nickel, and cobalt. The magnetic field strength (H) and the magnetic flux density (B) are related through a hysteresis loop.
Rubber and Polymers: Some rubber and polymer materials show hysteresis in their stress-strain curves, indicating energy loss during deformation.
Some Ceramics: Certain types of ceramics can exhibit hysteresis in their electrical properties under cyclic stress.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Linear Systems: Most linear systems do not exhibit hysteresis. Examples include simple resistors, capacitors, and inductors in electrical circuits.
Ideal Springs: An ideal spring follows Hooke's law perfectly, meaning it does not retain any memory of previous deformations.
Ideal Capacitors and Inductors: These components store and release energy without retaining any history of previous charge or current states.

Air:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis. It is a simple gas and follows the ideal gas laws under most conditions. The pressure and volume relationship for an ideal gas is described by Boyle's law, which is linear and does not depend on past states. However, in certain complex scenarios involving phase changes (like condensation and evaporation), air might exhibit some form of hysteresis, but this is not a fundamental property of air itself.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of specific materials and systems, particularly those that can store and release energy in a way that depends on their history. Air, being a simple gas, does not inherently exhibit hysteresis.
