What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state will "remember" past conditions even after those conditions have changed. Hysteresis is commonly seen in magnetic materials, rubber, and some types of mechanical systems.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: The magnetization of a ferromagnetic material depends on its history of being exposed to different magnetic fields. This is why a magnet can retain its magnetic properties even after the external magnetic field is removed.
Rubber: When stretched or compressed, rubber exhibits hysteresis due to the internal rearrangement of polymer chains. The energy required to stretch the rubber is greater than the energy released when it returns to its original shape.
Mechanical Systems: Some mechanical systems, like springs with friction, exhibit hysteresis because the force required to deform them depends on the direction and history of deformation.

Elements and Simple Substances:
Simple elements or pure substances generally do not exhibit hysteresis because they do not have complex internal structures or histories that would cause such behavior. For example, metals like copper or gold do not show significant hysteresis under normal conditions.

Air:
Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis. It is a simple gas composed of molecules (primarily nitrogen and oxygen) that do not retain any memory of past conditions. Changes in air pressure or temperature do not depend on the history of these changes; they respond directly to the current conditions.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems, particularly those with complex internal structures or histories. Simple elements and air do not exhibit hysteresis.
