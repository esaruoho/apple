Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time is influenced by its past states.

Examples of Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: When a magnetic material is exposed to a magnetic field, it can retain some magnetization even after the external field is removed. This is known as remanence.
Rubber and Springs: The deformation of rubber or the stretch of a spring depends on how much it was stretched previously. This is often seen in stress-strain curves.
Thermocouples: Some thermocouples exhibit hysteresis due to the temperature-dependent behavior of their materials.
Solenoids: The force exerted by a solenoid can depend on the previous current through it.

Elements and Substances Without Hysteresis:
Most Metals and Non-metals: These do not typically show significant hysteresis unless they are specifically engineered or subjected to specific conditions.
Air: Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis because it is a simple gas with no memory of past states. The properties of air (such as pressure, temperature, and density) change based on current conditions and do not retain a "history" of previous states.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
No, air does not have hysteresis. The properties of air such as pressure, temperature, and density change based on the current conditions and do not retain a "memory" of past states. For example, if you heat a volume of air, it will expand and cool down to its original temperature when you remove the heat, without retaining any "history" of the heating process.

In summary, hysteresis is a characteristic of certain materials and systems, particularly those involving magnetic fields, rubber-like materials, and some types of sensors. Air, being a simple gas, does not exhibit hysteresis.
