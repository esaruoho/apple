What has hysteresis? Elements etc. What does not have hysteresis? Does air have hysteresis?

Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials or systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state at any given time is influenced by its past states.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt. The magnetic field strength (H) and the resulting magnetization (M) do not immediately return to their initial values when the external magnetic field is removed; instead, they follow a loop known as the hysteresis loop.
Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel titanium alloy) can change shape in response to temperature changes and retain this shape until a certain threshold is reached. When the temperature is changed again, the material does not immediately revert to its original shape but follows a hysteresis loop.
Some Polymers: Certain types of polymers can show hysteresis in their mechanical properties under cyclic loading.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Most gases and fluids do not exhibit hysteresis because their properties (like pressure and volume) typically respond directly to the applied force without retaining a memory of previous states.
Pure metals and simple conductive materials generally do not show significant hysteresis unless they are subjected to specific conditions that induce it (e.g., through repeated cycles of stress).

Air:
Air, being a gas, does not exhibit hysteresis in the same way as materials with magnetic or mechanical memory. The properties of air (such as pressure and density) respond directly to changes in temperature and volume without retaining a history of previous states. Therefore, air does not have hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily observed in materials with memory effects, such as magnetic and shape-memory alloys, while gases like air do not exhibit this behavior.
