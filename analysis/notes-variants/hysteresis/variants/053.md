Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state depends on the path taken to reach that state, not just the current conditions.

Elements with Hysteresis:
Magnetic Materials: Many ferromagnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, including iron, nickel, and cobalt. The most common example is the magnetic hysteresis loop, which shows how the magnetization of a material changes with an applied magnetic field.
Rubber and Polymers: Some rubber and polymer materials can show mechanical hysteresis, meaning they store energy during deformation and release it slowly as they return to their original shape.
Some Electronic Components: Certain types of electronic components, like some types of capacitors and inductors, can exhibit hysteresis under specific operating conditions.

Elements without Hysteresis:
Most gases and fluids do not exhibit hysteresis because their properties (like pressure and volume) change smoothly and predictably with temperature and external forces.
Pure metals and simple conductive materials generally do not show significant hysteresis unless they undergo phase transitions or other complex behaviors.

Does Air Have Hysteresis?
Air, being a gas, does not typically exhibit hysteresis. The properties of air (such as density, pressure, and volume) change in a relatively smooth and predictable manner with changes in temperature and external pressure. While there might be some minor effects due to non-ideal gas behavior at very low temperatures or high pressures, these are not considered hysteresis in the strict sense.

In summary, hysteresis is more commonly associated with magnetic materials, certain polymers, and some electronic components, while gases like air do not exhibit this property under normal conditions.
