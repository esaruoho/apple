Hysteresis is a property observed in certain materials and systems where the output depends not only on the current input but also on the history of inputs. This means that the system's state or response lags behind the input, often forming a loop or memory effect.

Elements with Hysteresis:

Magnetic Materials: Many magnetic materials exhibit hysteresis, including iron, nickel, and cobalt. The most common example is ferrite, which is used in various electronic components due to its strong magnetic properties and hysteresis behavior.

Rubber and Polymers: Some rubber and polymer materials can show hysteresis under mechanical stress, particularly in their strain recovery.

Some Ferroelectric Materials: Certain ferroelectric materials can exhibit hysteresis in their polarization characteristics.

Shape Memory Alloys: Materials like nitinol (nickel titanium alloy) can show hysteresis in their phase transformations between different crystal structures.

Ceramic Materials: Some ceramic materials can exhibit hysteresis in their electrical properties under varying conditions.

Elements without Hysteresis:

Most simple elements and pure metals do not exhibit significant hysteresis under normal conditions. For example, copper, aluminum, and gold do not show pronounced hysteresis effects.

Air and Hysteresis:

Air itself does not exhibit hysteresis because it is a simple gas and does not have the complex internal structure or memory effects that materials like magnetic substances possess. However, certain properties of air, such as its thermal conductivity or specific heat, can change depending on past conditions, but this is not typically referred to as hysteresis.

In summary, hysteresis is primarily observed in materials with complex internal structures or memory effects, such as magnetic materials, some polymers, and shape memory alloys. Simple elements and gases like air do not exhibit hysteresis.
