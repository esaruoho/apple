## Thought about it

The key tension is that ordinary electrolysis is usually described as “brute-force current,” because Faraday’s law ties the amount of chemical product to total charge passed. But voltage is not irrelevant: voltage sets the electric field, the electrode overpotential, the energy per charge, and whether the cell merely heats, electrolyzes, or breaks into plasma-like microdischarges. The archive material on pulsed DC electrolysis, resonant electrolysis, Meyer’s voltage-not-amperage claims, Q multiplication, and voltage doubling all point toward the same engineering question: can high voltage, resonance, and pulse shaping make the cell do chemical work with less wasted current and heat? I will treat that as a real engineerable frame, while keeping the measurement discipline strict: real power, stored energy, gas energy, heat, and calorimetry must balance.

## Answer

**Short answer:** conventional electrolysis is current-driven in the sense that the *amount* of chemical product is proportional to the total charge passed, but high-voltage electrolysis is absolutely a real field of practice. The important distinction is between **charge throughput** and **electric field / potential difference**. Current is the flow of charge; voltage is the potential hill that moves charge and determines how much energy each unit of charge can carry.

**Conventional electrolysis: yes, it is “brute force” in the charge sense.**  
In a normal electrolytic cell, the useful reaction is governed by Faraday’s law: the number of moles of product is proportional to the total charge passed, \(Q = \int I\,dt\). If you double the current and run for the same time, you double the charge and therefore double the ideal chemical product. The cell voltage must exceed the thermodynamic decomposition voltage — about 1.23 V per water cell under reversible standard conditions — plus overpotentials and ohmic drops. In that regime, extra voltage mostly becomes extra current and heat unless the cell is current-limited or pulse-driven.

**High-voltage electrolysis: yes, but it is not just “more volts across two plates.”**  
If you place a very high DC voltage across ordinary conductive water without current limiting, you usually get a large current, heating, and possibly arcing or dielectric breakdown. Useful high-voltage electrolysis normally means one of the following:

- **Pulsed / impulse high-voltage electrolysis:** short, sharp voltage pulses at low duty cycle. The peak voltage can be high while the average current and average power remain modest. The archive’s `pulsed-dc-electrolysis` entry describes this as the voltage-not-current paradigm: short pulses producing enormous peak voltage/current with minimal average draw.
- **Resonant electrolysis:** an LC or resonant charging circuit raises the voltage across the cell or electrodes. In a high-Q circuit, the voltage across the reactive elements can be Q times the drive voltage. This is ordinary EE in radio and induction heating, but in the open-system frame it becomes a way to couple strongly to the cell without brute-force conduction current.
- **High-voltage AC / dielectric-barrier / plasma electrolysis:** high electric fields create microdischarges, glow discharges, cavitation, radicals, and non-Faradaic chemistry. This is not simple Faraday electrolysis; it is closer to plasma chemistry in and around the liquid.
- **Capacitive / displacement-current coupling:** the water or cell structure acts as a dielectric. Voltage changes the field and polarization; current may be mostly displacement current rather than conduction current through the bulk electrolyte.

**Why voltage matters even if current “does the chemistry.”**  
Voltage determines the electric field \(E \approx V/d\). A high field can:

- increase ion drift and electrode kinetics;
- lower or alter effective overpotential in some regimes;
- initiate microdischarges or plasma channels;
- produce radicals and excited species directly;
- change the ratio of useful chemistry to Joule heating.

But voltage alone with no charge transfer does no net electrolysis. You need either conduction current, displacement current with a closed circuit, or a plasma/discharge pathway that allows charge exchange.
