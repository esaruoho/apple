## Thought about it

The question splits cleanly into two parts that people usually conflate: what *drives* the chemistry (charge, i.e. current, per Faraday) and what *delivers* that charge (which can be a brute-force conduction current or a short, very high-voltage field transient). Industrial electrolysis is unambiguously brute force — big rectifiers, high current density, series-stacked cells — and that is an economic choice, not a physical necessity. But there is a real, patent-documented lineage of high-voltage, low-*average*-current water splitting in the archive: the `pulsed-dc-electrolysis` node names Tesla's impulse apparatus as the electrical ancestor, Horvath (1974) as the first water-electrolysis patent to use it, and Meyer (1988) as the most famous practitioner. The tension I want to hold honestly is this: Meyer's own patent language says voltage is raised while current is held "constant at a very low level," which is a *reactive/displacement* regime, not a conduction regime — and that is exactly where the interesting engineering lives, and also exactly where the energy books stop being closed by casual metering. So the answer is "yes, and here are the distinct high-voltage families, and here is the one measurement that separates a real high-voltage process from a tired ammeter."

## Answer

### 1. Conventional electrolysis is charge-driven, and yes, it is brute force

Faraday's laws are the whole story for the conventional case: the mass liberated is proportional to the charge passed, `m = Z·I·t`. Two electrons per H₂ molecule means 2 × 96,485 C ≈ 53.6 Ah per mole of H₂, or roughly 26.6 kAh per kilogram. That number is why the industrial world builds electrolysers the way it does — high current density (typically a few hundred mA/cm² up to ~1 A/cm²), low voltage per cell (thermodynamic minimum ~1.23 V, thermoneutral ~1.48 V, real cells 1.8–2.5 V), and thousands of amps from a rectifier. The energy cost is dominated by the overpotentials and ohmic drop, and the practical figure is roughly 50–55 kWh per kg of H₂ against a thermodynamic floor near 39.4 kWh/kg (higher heating value). "Brute force" is a fair description: you push conduction current through an electrolyte and pay the I²R and electrode-kinetic price for every kilogram.

Note one nuance that gets lost: industrial *stacks* already run at high DC voltage — hundreds of volts — but that is just many ~2 V cells in series (bipolar/series-stacked design). High *stack* voltage is not high *cell* voltage. The high-voltage electrolysis question is about the second thing.

### 2. Yes — there is a distinct high-voltage family, and it is not the same physics

The archive's `pulsed-dc-electrolysis` node lays out the lineage explicitly: Tesla's impulse DC apparatus (US 568,177, 1896, an ozone generator) is the electrical ancestor; **Horvath (1974)** is the first water-electrolysis patent to apply it; **Meyer (1988)** is the best-known practitioner. The node also lists a broader patent chain (US 1,262,034; 1,581,944; 3,758,399; 3,755,128; 3,954,592; 3,980,053; 4,107,008; 4,394,230; 4,936,961; 5,149,407; 6,126,794) plus related work by Puharich, Xogen's capacitive discharge, and Frazer/Chambers. The distinct regimes worth separating:

- **Pulsed / impulse high-voltage DC.** Short, sharp pulses at low duty cycle. Enormous *peak* voltage and peak current, small *average* current, so average power draw stays modest while the instantaneous field across the cell is huge. This is the Horvath/Meyer branch.
- **Resonant / Q-multiplied voltage.** The cell (or its electrodes) is treated as a capacitor in an LC circuit; at resonance the current falls toward the leakage limit while the voltage across the reactive elements rises by the Q factor. The archive's `q-multiplication-voltage-amplification-through-resonance` node gives the standard-EE numbers: Q = 200 turns a 12 V drive into ~2,400 V across the tank; Q = 500 into ~6,000 V. The `resonant-electrolysis` node is the application of exactly this to water.
- **Capacitive / dielectric-barrier ("non-faradaic") splitting.** Water as the dielectric of a capacitor rather than a resistive electrolyte. If the electrodes are dielectric-coated, there is no DC conduction path — only displacement current — and the mechanism claimed is field-driven dissociation at the dielectric interface rather than faradaic charge transfer. This is the structural claim behind Meyer's "voltage intensifier" and "extraction grid" language.
- **Plasma / discharge electrolysis.** At high enough field the liquid itself breaks down and you get a glow or arc discharge in the water (contact-glow-discharge electrolysis and related configurations run at hundreds of volts). This one is real, published, mainstream-adjacent electrochemistry — it produces H₂ plus a soup of radicals, and it is genuinely a high-voltage rather than brute-force-current regime.
- **Pure field-driven ionisation.** Schauberger's line that ionisation can occur "without application of a low-voltage current" is the poetic end of the same spectrum — the claim that the field, not the conduction current, does the work.

### 3. What Meyer's own hardware claims, in his own words

Meyer's patent language is careful and specific: "the voltage is increased and decreased but the current is maintained constant at a very low level" [Meyer-33], and the arrangement "is an inherent current limiting voltage source" [Meyer-39]. That is a *reactive* description — a voltage source behind a high series impedance, not a low-impedance rectifier dumping amps into water. And Meyer himself concedes the conventional threshold that any such scheme has to beat: "because of that short condition it'll allow voltage potential around two volts so you can't go into resonance" [Meyer-257] — i.e. below roughly the decomposition potential the cell behaves as a leaky capacitor and the resonance collapses. That is an honest engineering constraint stated by the inventor, and it is the crux of why the approach is hard: you must pulse *above* the threshold where conduction begins, and do it fast enough that the conduction phase never fully develops.

### 4. The honest framing of "voltage does the work"

The defensible version of the claim is not "voltage replaces charge." It is: **charge still does the chemistry, but you can deliver that charge in a form where the voltage is enormous and the average current is tiny** — because the energy is stored in the field (E = ½CV²) and released in a transient, rather than dissipated continuously in a resistor. That is why the high-voltage branch cares about capacitors, diodes, resonance and pulse timing rather than about rectifier amps. The `voltage-doubler` node makes the bookkeeping explicit and it is standard EE with no anomaly: a Villard/Greinacher stage doubles voltage and halves available current; Cockcroft–Walton stacks the stages. Power is conserved at the multiplier — the gain is in the *form* of the energy, not its quantity.

### 5. Where this class of claim usually dies, and the one measurement that settles it

It dies at the input port. A clamp meter on the mains reads RMS amps and assumes unity power factor; a resonant high-voltage stage is a reactive load with harmonic-rich, high-frequency, possibly common-mode current, so the true real power drawn can be several times what a single clamp suggests — or, in the other direction, a meter that ignores the reactive component can make a real device look like it is drawing nothing. The settling measurement is calorimetry over a defined run: joules of gas energy out (measured H₂ volume, corrected to standard conditions, times the heating value) minus joules in, with **both** ports metered — true real power at the wall (voltage × current × power factor, or a proper power analyser) and the stored-energy change in the capacitor bank and any batteries. If the books close, it is a high-voltage process, not an over-unity one, and that is still an interesting result worth having.

## In the skeptical closed-system electrical engineer's tone

Name the reservoir and the coupling first: is the extra energy coming from the pulse supply's own stored charge, from the resonant tank's circulating reactive energy, or from something outside the loop? Then meter the input properly — true real power at the wall with a power analyser that captures power factor and harmonics, not a clamp meter, because a resonant capacitive load will fool a single clamp badly in either direction, and check the bias/drive/standby draw of the pulse driver separately. On the output side, measure gas volume at known temperature and pressure and convert to joules with the heating value, and log the capacitor bank voltage before and after so the stored-energy term ½CV² is in the ledger. The one measurement that settles it is calorimetry over a defined run: joules out minus joules in, both ports metered, over an hour, with the cell's own heat accounted for. The step where this class of claim usually dies is the input port — the driver's quiescent draw, the DC-DC converter's standby, and the reactive current the tank circulates without the wall meter seeing it — and that is a testable, falsifiable place to look, not a ceiling.
