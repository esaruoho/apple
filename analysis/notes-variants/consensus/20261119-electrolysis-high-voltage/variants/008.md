## Answer

**Conventional electrolysis is current-driven (Faradaic).** Faraday's laws dictate that the moles of hydrogen produced are proportional to the total charge passed (coulombs = amperes × seconds). In a standard DC electrolyser, you apply a voltage above the thermodynamic decomposition potential (~1.23 V at 25 °C) plus overpotentials, and the current that flows — limited by solution resistance, electrode kinetics, and bubble coverage — determines the gas yield. Energy efficiency is capped by the ratio of the thermodynamic voltage to the actual cell voltage; typical industrial alkaline cells run 1.8–2.4 V/cell, giving 60–75 % LHV efficiency. This is what practitioners mean by "brute force": you push electrons through the electrolyte, and every electron splits one water molecule (2 e⁻ per H₂).

**High-voltage / pulsed / resonant electrolysis is a distinct paradigm.** The claim — made by Horvath (US 3,980,053, 1976), Meyer (US 4,613,779, 1986; US 4,798,661, 1989), Puharich, Xogen, and others — is that **voltage, not current, does the work**. The water cell is treated as a **capacitive dielectric** in a resonant charging-choke circuit. Key features from the verified record:

- **Meyer**: "the voltage is increased and decreased but the current is maintained constant at a very low level" [Meyer-33]; "hence the arrangement is an inherent current limiting voltage source" [Meyer-39]; "as you raise the voltage higher, then there were higher, more gas was being generated, not on a linear effect as to the electrolysis process" [Meyer-162].
- **Horvath (1974)**: First water-electrolysis patent to use Tesla-style impulse DC — short, high-voltage pulses at low duty cycle, producing enormous peak voltage with minimal average power draw.
- **Q multiplication**: A high-Q LC tank (Q = 200–500) driven at resonance amplifies the driving voltage by Q× across the capacitor (the cell). A 12 V drive becomes 2.4–6 kV across the water dielectric — standard EE, used in every radio receiver.
- **Voltage doubler / charge-pump rectification**: After Q multiplication, ultrafast diodes (e.g., UF5408, 75 ns recovery) in a Villard/Greinacher doubler capture the multiplied voltage as DC without loading the resonant tank — the same topology Don Smith uses to extract energy from ambient background voltage.
- **Non-Faradaic claims**: Proponents report gas yields exceeding Faraday predictions for the measured *average* input current, attributing the excess to field-driven bond cleavage, dielectric breakdown at the electrode/electrolyte interface, or vacuum/ambient energy coupling. Critics attribute apparent anomalies to unmeasured RMS current (harmonics, displacement current, RF pickup), calorimetric errors, or chemical energy from electrode corrosion.

**Engineering distinction**: In conventional electrolysis, the cell is a **resistive load** (V = IR + overpotential). In the high-voltage resonant approach, the cell is a **reactive element** (capacitor) in a tuned circuit — the impedance is dominated by capacitive reactance, and real current (ohmic loss) is minimised by design. The "brute force" vs. "voltage does the work" framing maps directly to **resistive vs. reactive drive**.

**Verification path**: To settle any specific device, measure **real power in (all ports, DC + AC + RF, calibrated calorimetry or true-RMS wattmeter with bandwidth > 10× fundamental)** vs. **chemical energy out (gas volume × LHV, corrected for temperature/pressure)** over ≥ 1 hour. The step where this class of claim usually dies: **unmetered harmonic/RF current on the drive lines, or probe placement that misses common-mode displacement current through the cell's stray capacitance to ground**.
