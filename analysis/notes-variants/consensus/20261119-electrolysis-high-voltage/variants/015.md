## Answer

**Short answer:** Conventional electrolysis is current-driven (Faradaic) — you pass DC current through an electrolyte, and the amount of hydrogen/oxygen produced is stoichiometrically locked to the total charge passed (Faraday's laws). This is the "brute force" method: low voltage (1.8–2.5 V per cell), high current, significant resistive (I²R) heating, and practical efficiencies of 60–80% HHV.

**High-voltage / pulsed / resonant electrolysis** is a different paradigm, documented in a lineage of patents and experiments:

| **Xogen / capacitive discharge** | Capacitive discharge into water cell; peak power high, average power low | Various |

**Meyer's specific circuit** (per his patents and lectures): a resonant charging choke (bifilar inductor) + water capacitor cell + blocking diode + voltage intensifier. At resonance, the voltage across the cell rises (Q × drive voltage) while the input current falls toward leakage. The claim: the electric field across the water dielectric does the bond-breaking; the hydrogen is "released, not generated" [Meyer-39, Meyer-257].

**Where the physics diverges:** Conventional electrochemistry says the reaction rate is proportional to current (Faraday). High-voltage proponents argue the field can polarise and rupture the H–O bond directly (dielectric breakdown / field emission / tunnelling), bypassing the electrode overpotentials that waste energy as heat. No peer-reviewed replication has confirmed gas output exceeding Faraday's law under controlled calorimetry — but the *mechanism* (high field, low average current) is electrically real and distinct from brute-force DC.

**Practical note:** If you build a high-voltage pulse circuit (e.g., Meyer-style VIC), you must measure: (1) real input power (DC source, not just RMS voltage × current), (2) gas volume per unit time, (3) calorimetry of the cell. The "one measurement that would settle it" is joules-in vs. joules-out (gas HHV + heat) over an hour, both ports metered. That is where this class of claim usually dies — unmetered reactive power, probe placement errors, or unaccounted bias supply draw.

**Device-level test that would settle it:**
1. **Meter the DC source** — not AC RMS, not "input voltage × current." Use a precision shunt or Hall sensor on the battery lead, logged at ≥10× the switching frequency. Capture the supply's own draw, bias, drive, standby.
