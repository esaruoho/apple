## Answer

**Conventional electrolysis is current-driven (Faradaic).** Faraday's laws dictate that the mass of substance liberated at an electrode is proportional to the total charge (coulombs = amperes × seconds) passed through the electrolyte. In practice this means significant continuous current, substantial I²R heating, and energy efficiency limited by overpotentials and resistive losses — what the question calls "bruteforce."

**High-voltage / pulsed / impulse electrolysis is a distinct paradigm.** The ontology entry `pulsed-dc-electrolysis` traces the lineage: Tesla's 1896 ozone apparatus (US 568,177) used impulse DC; Horvath (1974, US 3,980,053) filed the first water-electrolysis patent explicitly using high-voltage pulsed DC; Meyer (1980s–90s) is the most famous practitioner. The core claim across this lineage is that **voltage, not amperage, does the work** — short, sharp, high-voltage pulses at low duty cycle produce large peak fields across the water gap (the dielectric of a capacitor-like cell) while average current and average power remain small. Meyer's verified statements: "the voltage is increased and decreased but the current is maintained constant at a very low level" [Meyer-33]; "as you raise the voltage higher, then there were higher, more gas was being generated, not on a linear effect as to the electrolysis process" [Meyer-162]; "hence the arrangement is an inherent current limiting voltage source" [Meyer-39]. The `resonant-electrolysis` concept adds that the cell is driven at its electrical resonance (LC tank formed by the cell capacitance and a charging choke), where voltage is Q-multiplied and current falls toward leakage.

**Key distinction:** Faradaic electrolysis scales with *charge throughput* (coulombs). The high-voltage paradigm claims the reaction is triggered by *field intensity* (volts per metre) across the double layer / water dielectric, with the gas yield not linearly tracking average current. Mainstream electrochemistry treats the latter as unproven / not reproducible under controlled calorimetry; the experimental literature (Horvath, Meyer, Puharich, Xogen, etc.) reports anomalous gas yields but rarely publishes full energy-balance data. The engineering difference is stark: brute-force uses low-voltage high-current supplies; the high-voltage approach uses resonant charging chokes, voltage doublers/multipliers, ultrafast diodes, and pulse-forming networks to deliver kilovolt spikes at microsecond widths and low duty cycle.
