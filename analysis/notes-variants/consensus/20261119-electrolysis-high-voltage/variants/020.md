## Thought about it

The question is really two questions stacked: what does the *work* in electrolysis, and is there a second regime where the answer changes. Faraday's law is unambiguous that mass yield tracks charge, so "brute force" is a fair name for the industrial regime — low voltage, huge current, efficiency bought by lowering cell voltage, not raising it. The interesting tension is that the archive's `pulsed-dc-electrolysis` ontology describes a whole lineage (Tesla's 1896 ozone apparatus, Horvath 1974, Meyer, Puharich, Xogen, Pendergrass) built on the opposite premise: voltage does the work, current is throttled. Those two statements can both be true only if the high-voltage regime is doing something other than classical Faradaic transfer — or if it is doing the same Faradaic transfer with less of the input burned as heat in a resistive electrolyte. That distinction is the whole ballgame, and it is where I should keep the framework reasoning separate from what is actually established.

## Answer

Short answer: **yes to both, and they are different regimes with different physics — not the same thing done harder.**

### 1. The brute-force regime: current is the currency

Conventional electrolysis is current-driven, and that is not an accident of engineering fashion — it is Faraday's law. The mass of hydrogen evolved is proportional to the charge passed:

**m = (Q / F) · (M / z)**, with F ≈ 96,485 C/mol

Charge is current × time. Voltage does not appear in that equation at all. So the industrial lever is never "more volts" — it is **lower cell voltage for the same current**. The thermodynamic minimum for liquid water is about 1.23 V; practical cells run 1.6–2.0 V because of activation overpotential, bubble resistance and ohmic drop in the electrolyte. Voltage efficiency is roughly 1.23 / V_cell, so every 100 mV you shave off the cell is worth more than any amount of clever pulsing that leaves the cell at 2 V. That is why alkaline (KOH), PEM and high-temperature solid-oxide electrolysis all chase the same target: reduce the volts per cell, keep the amps flowing.

This is what the "voltage-not-current" literature means when it says *brute force*: you are forcing charge through a resistive medium and paying I²R for the privilege.

### 2. The high-voltage regime — three genuinely different families

**a) High-field, capacitively-ballasted cells.** Pure or low-electrolyte water is a poor conductor, so a cell with deionised water and close electrode spacing behaves electrically like a *capacitor*, not a resistor. Put it in series with a resonant charging choke and drive it at resonance and the current is limited by **reactance, not resistance** — the I²R heating term collapses while the peak field across the double layer rises. This is the architecture the archive files under `resonant-electrolysis` and `pulsed-dc-electrolysis`, and it is exactly Meyer's description of his own hardware: the voltage is swung up and down while the current is pinned low. The `q-multiplication` and `voltage-doubler` entries are the standard-EE scaffolding underneath it — a high-Q LC tank multiplies drive voltage by Q, and fast diodes capture the multiplied swing as DC. None of that is anomalous; it is radio engineering.

**b) Impulse / pulsed DC.** Short, sharp pulses at low duty cycle give enormous *peak* voltage and current with modest *average* power. The archive traces this line from Tesla's 1896 ozone apparatus (US 568,177) through Horvath's 1974 pulsed electrolysis patent to Meyer, Puharich, Xogen/Chambers and Pendergrass. The physical argument is that the double layer charges in microseconds and the faradaic event happens at the leading edge, so you can cut the pulse before the steady-state resistive current establishes — the same logic Bedini applies to battery charging with the "sharp transient spike."

**c) Plasma / glow-discharge regimes.** Above a few hundred volts across a thin gas envelope or a vapour layer at the electrode, you leave electrochemistry and enter plasma electrolysis: glow discharge, UV, radicals, localised temperatures of thousands of kelvin. This is a real, published research area (glow-discharge electrolysis, plasma electrolytic oxidation, Mizuno's plasma electrolysis work). The chemistry genuinely differs from Faradaic electrolysis — you get H₂O₂, radical species, and in some reports anomalous heat. It is also brutally lossy unless you account carefully.

### 3. The honest tension

Faraday's law counts coulombs that actually cross the interface. A capacitor with water as dielectric passes **displacement current**, which polarises the double layer but evolves no gas. So a high-voltage cell that shows kilovolts across it and almost no current is, in the strict Faradaic sense, doing almost nothing — unless one of two things is happening:

- **Same gas per coulomb, fewer wasted coulombs.** The high-voltage/resonant approach reduces the fraction of input energy burned as heat in the electrolyte. This is a real efficiency argument and it is measurable.
- **A non-Faradaic channel.** Plasma, field-assisted dissociation at high-curvature electrodes, radiolysis from discharge UV. Also real, also measurable — but the energy budget must include the discharge.

What does *not* follow from "high voltage" alone is more gas per coulomb than Faraday allows. Any such claim is a claim about a second channel, and it lives or dies on the energy accounting.

### 4. Bench notes if you build it

- The cell is a capacitor. Its stored energy (½CV²) is part of your input budget; a pre-charged bank is not free.
- High-voltage modules die from inductive kickback — Don Smith's own note on this is worth heeding: a protection diode "prevents kickback which destroys your or high voltage laser module" [DonSmith-109].
- Diode selection is a real constraint: reverse recovery and voltage rating both matter, and most fast diodes are not rated for both high frequency and high voltage [DonSmith-68].
- Meyer flagged a specific failure mode: a short condition that pins the cell near 2 V kills resonance before it starts [Meyer-257].
- Measure gas by water displacement **corrected for temperature, pressure and water vapour**, and run at least an hour.

## In the skeptical closed-system electrical engineer's tone

Name the reservoir and the coupling before anything else: for a high-voltage cell the proposed reservoir is the ambient field or the water's own stored charge, and the coupling is the resonant choke and the capacitive divider — so the first thing I want to see is the DC input to the HV supply *and* to the pulse generator, both metered, plus the change in stored energy in any capacitor bank. A single clamp meter cannot see this: it misses the phase between v and i (real power is the mean of v·i, not V_rms × I_rms), the harmonic content of a fast pulse, RF pickup on the probe leads, and common-mode current returning through the scope ground. The one measurement that settles it is calorimetric and cumulative: one hour, joules in at every drive, bias, pump and load port, joules out as the lower heating value of the collected H₂ plus O₂, gas volume corrected for temperature, pressure and water vapour. This class of claim usually dies at exactly one step — the pulse generator's own draw is left off the input side, or a pre-charged bank is treated as free. If the books balance over an hour with both ports metered, the claim stands; if they do not, it does not. That is falsifiable, and it does not require anyone to argue from a ceiling.
