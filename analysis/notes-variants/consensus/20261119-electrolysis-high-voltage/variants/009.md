## Answer

**Conventional (Faraday) electrolysis is current-driven.** You pass DC through an electrolyte; the gas volume is proportional to total charge (coulombs) per Faraday's laws. Minimum thermodynamic voltage is 1.23 V; practical cells run 1.8–2.0 V with significant overpotentials. Efficiency is limited by resistive (I²R) heating and overvoltage losses. This is the industrial standard — "brute force" in the sense that amperage does the chemical work.

**High-voltage / pulsed-DC electrolysis is a different paradigm.** The lineage runs: Tesla's impulse DC (US 568,177, 1896 ozone apparatus) → Horvath (US 3,980,053, 1974, pulsed electrolysis cell) → Puharich (1980s, high-frequency AC/pulsed) → Meyer (1980s–90s, Water Fuel Cell, resonant charging choke, water as dielectric capacitor) → Xogen (capacitive discharge). The claim across this lineage: **voltage, not amperage, performs the dissociation**; current is minimized or appears only as brief displacement/transient spikes; the cell is driven at resonance so voltage multiplies (Q-factor) while average input power stays low. Meyer's patents (e.g., US 4,613,779, US 4,798,661) describe a "voltage intensifier circuit" with charging chokes, a water capacitor cell, and a resonant feedback loop — "the voltage is increased and decreased but the current is maintained constant at a very low level" [Meyer-33]. Horvath's patent uses microsecond HV pulses across a gap. Puharich used 600 Hz + harmonics. None of these have been independently replicated with rigorous calorimetry showing net energy gain over conventional electrolysis; the mainstream view attributes reported excess gas to measurement artifacts (unmetered current paths, RF pickup, gas volume errors). The skeptical test: simultaneous joule-in / joule-out calorimetry over hours, all ports metered (DC, RF, ground), gas volume by mass flow or displaced water at STP, Faraday efficiency calculated. Until that closes, the high-voltage claims remain unvalidated in open literature.
