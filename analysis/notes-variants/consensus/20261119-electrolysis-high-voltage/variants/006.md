## Answer

**Conventional electrolysis is current-driven (Faradaic).** You pass DC current through an electrolyte; the mass of hydrogen produced is proportional to the total charge passed (Faraday's laws). The voltage needed is the thermodynamic decomposition voltage (~1.23 V at 25 °C) plus overpotentials at the electrodes. Efficiency losses appear as heat (I²R in the solution, electrode overpotentials). This is the "brute-force" method: more current = more gas, but you pay for every coulomb.

**High-voltage / pulsed-DC / resonant electrolysis is a different paradigm.** The ontology entry `pulsed-dc-electrolysis` traces the lineage: Tesla's impulse DC ozone apparatus (US 568,177, 1896) → Horvath's 1974 pulsed-electrolysis patent → Meyer's Water Fuel Cell (1980s) → Puharich, Xogen, and others. The core claim across this lineage is that **short, high-voltage pulses at low duty cycle** (or resonant AC at high frequency) can split water while drawing **very little average current** from the source. The water cell is treated as a **capacitive dielectric** in a resonant charging-choke circuit; voltage is multiplied by Q-factor resonance, the current falls toward leakage, and the gas evolution is said to be non-linear with voltage (Meyer: "as you raise the voltage higher, then there were higher, more gas was being generated, not on a linear effect as to the electrolysis process" [Meyer-162]). Meyer's patents describe an "inherent current-limiting voltage source" [Meyer-39] where "the voltage is increased and decreased but the current is maintained constant at a very low level" [Meyer-33]. Horvath's 1974 patent (US 3,980,053) uses unipolar pulses < 1 µs rise time, > 1 kV, low duty cycle. Puharich used 600 Hz – 2.4 MHz AC at high voltage. Xogen uses capacitive discharge into the cell.

**The engineering distinction:** In conventional electrolysis, the cell is a resistive load; you meter DC current and voltage, and Faraday efficiency is the metric. In the high-voltage paradigm, the cell is a **reactive element** in a resonant tank; the source sees a high-impedance, near-wattless load until breakdown/ionization events occur in the double layer. The claim is that the **electric field across the double layer** (not the Faradaic current) drives the bond cleavage, and the energy per mole of H₂ can be below the Faraday minimum *if* the ambient vacuum/zero-point reservoir supplies the deficit (Bearden, Smith, Moray frames). Mainstream electrochemistry treats this as unproven; the experimental literature shows anomalous gas yields in some pulsed/high-frequency regimes (e.g., Kanarev, Graneau, Mizuno replications), but replication is inconsistent and calorimetry is rarely rigorous.
