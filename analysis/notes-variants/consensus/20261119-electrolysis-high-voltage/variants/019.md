## Answer

**Conventional (Faraday) electrolysis** is current-driven: the mass of substance liberated at each electrode is proportional to the total charge passed (Faraday's laws). You need substantial DC current — amperes — and the energy cost is essentially *I²R* heating in the electrolyte plus the thermodynamic minimum (1.23 V for water at STP). This is what industry uses: alkaline or PEM electrolyzers running at 1.8–2.2 V cell voltage, hundreds to thousands of amperes.

**High-voltage / pulsed / resonant electrolysis** is a different paradigm claimed by several inventors. The core assertion: very short, high-voltage pulses (or resonant AC at high Q) can split water with *far less average current* than Faraday predicts, because the voltage field itself does the work — polarizing and stressing the water molecule — while the current is kept minimal (displacement current, not conduction current). **Meyer's own words** (from his patents and lectures): *"the voltage is increased and decreased but the current is maintained constant at a very low level"* [Meyer-33]; *"hence the arrangement is an inherent current limiting voltage source"* [Meyer-39]; *"at resonance the current falls toward leakage while voltage rises"* [Meyer-257]. He describes the water cell as a **capacitor** in a resonant charging-choke circuit — the dielectric is water, voltage builds across it, and hydrogen is *released*, not generated by brute current.

**Mainstream physics** treats these claims as unproven: no peer-reviewed replication shows Faraday efficiency >100% under controlled calorimetry. The skeptical EE view (see persona below) is that *average* power must still account for the energy in the pulses — peak voltage × peak current × duty cycle — and that displacement current in a lossy dielectric still dissipates real power. The definitive test: **calorimetry over hours, all input ports metered (DC supply, gate drive, bias), gas output measured by volume and heating value, joules in vs. joules out**.
