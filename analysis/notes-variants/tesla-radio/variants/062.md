Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, particularly as it was being developed and applied by others during his time. Tesla's work on wireless transmission and his claims about the nature of radio waves were rooted in his belief that the technology could be used for more than just communication over short distances.

Tesla's primary concerns with the electromagnetic radiation theory, as it was understood at the time, centered around the idea that radio waves were simply a form of electromagnetic radiation that could propagate through space without wires. He believed that this view was overly simplistic and did not fully capture the potential of wireless technology.

In his view, wireless transmission should not rely solely on the propagation of electromagnetic waves but should also involve a more direct interaction between the transmitter and receiver. Tesla proposed that the Earth itself played a crucial role in wireless transmission, an idea that is now known as "Earth resonance" or "resonant antenna systems."

Tesla's experiments and theories suggested that by properly tuning the frequency of the transmitter to the natural resonant frequency of the Earth, one could achieve much greater range and efficiency in wireless communication. This concept was quite different from the then-prevailing view that radio waves could travel through space independently of any physical medium.

While Tesla's ideas were revolutionary and ahead of their time, they were not widely accepted during his lifetime. It wasn't until later that other scientists and engineers began to explore and validate some of Tesla's concepts, particularly the use of resonant frequencies in wireless transmission.

It's important to note that Tesla's contributions to the field of wireless technology were immense, even if his specific theories about Earth resonance were not immediately embraced by the scientific community. His work laid the groundwork for modern wireless communication technologies, including radio, television, and cellular networks.
