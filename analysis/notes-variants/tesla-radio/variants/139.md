Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism and criticism towards the electromagnetic radiation theory as proposed by Heinrich Hertz. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, which were understood as short-range, line-of-sight signals, did not fully capture the nature of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were based on his own experiments and theories, particularly those involving resonant circuits and long-distance transmission. He argued that the propagation of electromagnetic waves over long distances required a different approach than what Hertzian waves suggested. Tesla believed that the Earth itself played a crucial role in the transmission and reception of these waves, a concept known as "earth resonance."

In his writings and lectures, Tesla often criticized the prevailing scientific understanding of the time, which was heavily influenced by Hertz's work. He contended that the idea of radio waves being transmitted through the air without any medium or "ether" was incomplete and did not explain the phenomena he observed in his experiments.

For example, Tesla demonstrated that radio waves could be transmitted over vast distances using resonant circuits, which could resonate with the natural frequency of the Earth. This resonant circuit theory was fundamentally different from the Hertzian wave theory, which did not account for such long-distance transmission without direct line-of-sight.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the development of radio technology, they were also controversial at the time. The scientific community largely accepted Hertz's theory, which laid the groundwork for modern radio communication. However, Tesla's contributions to the field, including his patents and demonstrations, have been recognized and celebrated in later years.

In summary, Tesla's claims about the inappropriateness of the Hertzian wave theory for understanding wireless communication were rooted in his experimental observations and theoretical framework, which emphasized the role of the Earth and resonant circuits in long-distance transmission.
