Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism about the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work on radio waves. Tesla believed that the concept of "Hertzian waves" did not fully capture the essence of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission involved a more direct and efficient method than what was being described by the prevailing scientific theories of his time. He argued that the transmission of information through the air could be achieved without the need for a physical medium like the oscillating circuits proposed by Hertz. Instead, Tesla thought that the Earth itself could act as a conductor and that wireless signals could travel through it directly.

In his patents and writings, Tesla described a system where the Earth and the atmosphere acted as a single conducting entity, allowing for the transmission of signals over vast distances. This idea was encapsulated in his concept of "earth resonance," which suggested that certain frequencies could resonate through the Earth and the ionosphere, effectively creating a global network for wireless communication.

Tesla's claims often clashed with the established scientific community of his era, which was more aligned with the Hertzian wave theory. However, his ideas laid the groundwork for many modern technologies, including long-distance radio transmission and wireless communication systems.

It's important to note that while Tesla's theories were innovative and influential, they were also complex and sometimes difficult to prove scientifically at the time. It wasn't until later that advancements in technology and further research confirmed aspects of Tesla's ideas, particularly in the realm of long-distance radio communication and the use of the Earth as a conductor for wireless signals.
