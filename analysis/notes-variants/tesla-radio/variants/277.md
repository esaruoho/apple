Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, particularly as it was being developed and applied during his time. Tesla's work on radio communication predates many of the theoretical developments attributed to Heinrich Hertz, who is often credited with the discovery of electromagnetic waves.

Tesla believed that the concept of "Hertzian waves" or electromagnetic radiation as understood at the time was not fully capturing the essence of wireless communication. He argued that the transmission of radio signals required a different approach than what was being proposed by the Hertzian wave theory. Specifically, Tesla emphasized the importance of resonant circuits and the idea that antennas should be tuned to resonate with the frequency of the signal being transmitted. This resonant approach was central to his design of the Wardenclyffe Tower, which he intended to demonstrate long-distance wireless transmission.

Tesla's views were based on his experimental observations and theoretical insights, which differed from the emerging scientific consensus of his era. He believed that the key to effective wireless communication lay in the precise tuning of the transmitter and receiver to the same frequency, rather than simply radiating energy into the air. This resonant approach was more aligned with his understanding of how electrical systems interact and how energy could be efficiently transferred over distances.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and influential, they also led to some controversy and misunderstanding. The scientific community at the time was still developing a comprehensive understanding of electromagnetic phenomena, and Tesla's claims sometimes clashed with the established theories of his contemporaries.

In summary, Tesla's criticisms of the Hertzian wave theory were rooted in his belief that the proper understanding and application of wireless communication required a more nuanced approach that emphasized resonance and tuning, rather than the simple propagation of electromagnetic waves.
