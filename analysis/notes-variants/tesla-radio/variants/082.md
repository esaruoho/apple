Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, particularly as it was being developed and applied by others during his time. Tesla's work on wireless transmission and his claims about the limitations of the Hertzian wave theory are well-documented.

Tesla believed that the concept of electromagnetic waves propagating through space in a manner similar to those described by Heinrich Hertz was not sufficient for understanding or achieving true wireless communication. He argued that the idea of waves traveling through the air was overly simplistic and did not capture the full nature of what he perceived as wireless energy transmission.

Tesla's approach emphasized the use of resonant circuits and the concept of "resonance" between transmitter and receiver. He believed that by tuning these circuits to resonate at the same frequency, the energy could be efficiently transferred without the need for the waves to travel through the air. This is fundamentally different from the Hertzian wave theory, which describes electromagnetic waves as propagating through space.

In his later years, Tesla continued to assert that the Hertzian wave theory was inadequate for practical wireless communication. He claimed that it led to inefficiencies and that true wireless transmission required a more sophisticated understanding of electrical resonance and the properties of matter.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the field of wireless technology, they were also controversial and not widely accepted at the time. Modern understanding of wireless communication has evolved beyond both Tesla's and Hertz's theories, incorporating elements of both and much more advanced concepts.

Today, we understand that wireless communication involves the propagation of electromagnetic waves through the air, but Tesla's emphasis on resonance and his skepticism towards the Hertzian wave theory have inspired ongoing research into more efficient and targeted methods of wireless energy transfer.
