Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work on radio waves. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, or what we now call radio waves, did not fully capture the essence of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's ideas were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission could be achieved through a medium that was more akin to a conductor than a simple wave propagating through space. He proposed that the Earth itself could act as a conductor and that wireless signals could be transmitted through the Earth's natural resonant frequency, a concept often referred to as "earth resonance" or "resonance theory."

In contrast, the prevailing view at the time, based on Hertz's experiments, suggested that radio waves traveled through the air without needing a medium. Tesla argued that this approach was incomplete and that it did not account for the efficiency and range of wireless communication that he observed and achieved.

Some key points from Tesla's perspective include:

Earth as a Conductor: Tesla believed that the Earth could be used as a conductor for wireless signals, which would allow for greater range and efficiency.
Resonance: He emphasized the importance of resonant frequencies in wireless transmission, suggesting that signals could be tuned to specific frequencies to enhance their propagation.
Wireless Power Transmission: Tesla also proposed the idea of wireless power transmission, which aligns with his broader vision of using the Earth as a conductor for both information and energy.

While Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the field of wireless technology, they were not widely accepted during his lifetime. It wasn't until later that many of these concepts were validated and incorporated into modern wireless technology.

It's important to note that Tesla's contributions to wireless communication are recognized today, but the way he conceptualized and described wireless phenomena differed from the established scientific understanding of his time.
