Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, particularly as it was being developed and popularized by others during his time. Tesla's claims about the incompatibility of the electromagnetic radiation theory (often referred to as the Hertzian wave theory after Heinrich Hertz) with his conception of wireless communication were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission could be achieved through a different mechanism.

Tesla's primary contention was that the radio waves described by Hertz were not the best way to transmit information over long distances. He believed that the Earth itself could be used as a conductor for wireless signals, a concept now known as "earth resonance" or "resonant antenna systems."

In his view, the Hertzian waves were too weak and dissipative, requiring large amounts of energy to transmit any meaningful signal. Tesla proposed that by using resonant frequencies, one could achieve much more efficient and powerful wireless communication. This idea was based on his experiments with high-frequency alternating currents and his observations of how these currents interacted with the Earth and the ionosphere.

While Tesla's ideas were innovative and have since been validated in part, they were also ahead of their time and not fully understood at the time. It took decades for scientists to develop the technology to effectively harness the principles Tesla suggested. Today, we understand that both methods—using the atmosphere for radio waves and using the Earth as a conductor—play roles in modern wireless communication, though the practical implementation often relies more heavily on the former.

It's important to note that Tesla's contributions to the field of wireless communication are immense, even if some of his specific theories about the nature of radio waves were not entirely correct. His work laid the groundwork for much of what we take for granted today in terms of wireless technology.
