Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism about the electromagnetic radiation theory as it was understood during his time, particularly regarding its application to wireless communication. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, which were named after Heinrich Hertz who demonstrated the existence of electromagnetic waves, did not fully capture the nature of wireless transmission as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless communication should be based on a system of resonant circuits rather than the propagation of electromagnetic waves through space. He argued that the idea of Hertzian waves being transmitted through the air was an oversimplification and that the true mechanism involved the resonance between the transmitter and receiver.

In his patent applications and public lectures, Tesla described his wireless systems as involving "resonance" or "sympathetic vibration," where the transmitter and receiver would resonate at the same frequency, allowing for efficient energy transfer without the need for the waves to travel through the air. This concept is somewhat similar to modern ideas of near-field communication, although Tesla's ideas were much broader and more ambitious.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and ahead of their time, they were not widely accepted during his lifetime. The scientific community and practical applications of wireless communication largely relied on the Hertzian wave theory until later developments in the 20th century confirmed many of Tesla's earlier observations and theories.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's theories play roles in wireless communication, with Hertzian waves being the primary means of transmitting information over longer distances, while Tesla's concepts of resonant circuits have found applications in various specialized areas of technology.
