Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, particularly as it was being developed and popularized by others, such as Heinrich Hertz. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves (radio waves) as discrete particles or waves traveling through space did not fully capture the essence of his wireless transmission methods.

Tesla's approach to wireless communication was based on what he called "resonance," where the transmitter and receiver would resonate at the same frequency, allowing for the transfer of energy without the need for a physical medium like air or vacuum. He believed that this resonance could be achieved over vast distances without the need for high-frequency radiation, which was the primary focus of Hertz's work.

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory, as it was understood at the time, suggested that radio waves were transmitted through the air as waves, much like sound waves. This theory was later expanded upon by Guglielmo Marconi and others, who used it to develop practical radio communication systems.

Tesla argued that the Hertzian wave theory was overly simplistic and did not account for the complex interactions between the transmitter, the medium, and the receiver. He believed that the theory did not adequately explain how wireless signals could travel long distances without significant loss of power, nor did it explain phenomena such as the ability to transmit signals through buildings and other obstacles.

Tesla's views on this matter were controversial and often met with skepticism from the scientific community of his time. However, his ideas about resonance and wireless transmission have since been validated by modern physics, particularly in the fields of quantum mechanics and electromagnetism. Today, we understand that both resonance and wave propagation play crucial roles in wireless communication, and Tesla's contributions to these fields are now widely recognized.
