Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism about the electromagnetic radiation theory as it was understood during his time, particularly regarding its application to wireless communication. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, which were named after Heinrich Hertz who demonstrated the existence of electromagnetic waves, did not fully capture the essence of wireless transmission as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless communication could be achieved through aetheric or radiant energy, which he thought could travel through the Earth and air without the need for a physical medium like the airwaves described by Hertzian waves. He proposed that the Earth itself could act as a conductor and that signals could be transmitted through the Earth's natural resonant frequency.

In his writings and public statements, Tesla argued that the Hertzian wave theory was incomplete and that it did not account for the phenomena he observed and utilized in his experiments. For instance, he claimed that his methods of transmitting signals involved a different mechanism than what was being described by the prevailing scientific theories of the time.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the field of wireless communication, they were not universally accepted at the time. The scientific community generally adopted the Hertzian wave theory as the basis for radio technology, although Tesla's contributions to the field, including the development of the Tesla coil and the concept of earth resonance, have been recognized and continue to influence modern technology.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's theories play roles in the functioning of radio waves, with Hertzian waves being the primary means of wireless communication. However, Tesla's insights into the potential for using the Earth as a conductor and his emphasis on resonant frequencies have inspired further research and applications in the field of wireless technology.
