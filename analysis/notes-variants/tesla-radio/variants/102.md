Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism and disagreement with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, which was primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work. Tesla believed that his own experiments and observations indicated that wireless communication could be achieved through a different mechanism than what Hertz had described.

Tesla's claims were based on his extensive work with high-frequency alternating currents and his belief in the existence of "earth currents" or "space currents," which he thought could carry signals without the need for a line-of-sight transmission. He proposed that these currents could propagate through the Earth and the ionosphere, effectively creating a global network for wireless communication.

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory, which was gaining acceptance at the time, suggested that radio waves traveled through space as waves of electromagnetic energy. Tesla argued that this theory did not fully capture the nature of wireless communication and that it was overly simplistic. He believed that the true mechanism involved a more complex interaction between the Earth and the ionosphere, and that the concept of radio waves traveling through empty space was an oversimplification.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the field of wireless technology, they were not universally accepted during his lifetime. The scientific community eventually came to accept the Hertzian wave theory as the basis for radio communication, although Tesla's contributions to the field are now widely recognized.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's theories have elements that contribute to our current understanding of wireless communication. Tesla's work laid the groundwork for many aspects of modern wireless technology, including the use of ground waves and sky waves in radio propagation.
