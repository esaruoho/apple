Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, particularly as it was being developed and popularized by others, such as Heinrich Hertz. Tesla believed that the concept of "Hertzian waves" or electromagnetic waves propagating through space did not fully capture the nature of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's conception of wireless technology was rooted in his belief that the Earth itself could be used as a conductor for transmitting information. He proposed that wireless signals could be transmitted through the Earth's ionosphere and the ground, rather than relying solely on the propagation of electromagnetic waves through the air. This idea is often referred to as "earth resonance" or "resonance theory."

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory, which was gaining acceptance during Tesla's time, suggested that electromagnetic waves could travel through the air without needing a physical medium. This theory was supported by experiments conducted by Hertz, which demonstrated the existence of electromagnetic waves.

Tesla's claims that the Hertzian wave theory was inimical to the proper understanding of wireless processes were based on his observations and experiments. He argued that the Hertzian wave theory did not account for the long-distance transmission capabilities that he observed and believed in. Tesla's experiments with resonant circuits and his development of the Wardenclyffe Tower project were aimed at demonstrating his theories of wireless transmission.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas about wireless communication were innovative and influential, they were also controversial and not widely accepted at the time. The scientific community largely embraced the Hertzian wave theory, which eventually became the foundation for modern radio technology.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's theories have their merits and applications. Modern wireless technology incorporates elements from both approaches, using the ionosphere and the Earth's surface as conductors for certain types of communication, while also utilizing electromagnetic waves for other applications.
