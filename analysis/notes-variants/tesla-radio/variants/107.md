Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism about the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work on radio waves. Tesla believed that the concept of "Hertzian waves" or electromagnetic waves propagating through space did not fully capture the essence of his wireless transmission methods.

Tesla's approach to wireless communication was rooted in his belief in aetheric or radiant energy, which he thought could be transmitted without the need for a physical medium like air or vacuum. He proposed that energy could be transmitted through the Earth and the atmosphere itself, a concept sometimes referred to as "earth resonance" or "resonant antenna systems."

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory focused on the propagation of electromagnetic waves through free space, which was a more straightforward interpretation of Maxwell's equations. Tesla argued that this theory was insufficient because it did not account for the phenomena he observed, such as the ability to transmit signals over long distances without relying solely on line-of-sight or direct propagation.

Tesla's claims were based on his experimental observations and theoretical speculations. He conducted numerous experiments to demonstrate what he called "wireless transmission," which involved sending signals through the ground and the air without the need for a physical conductor. His patents and demonstrations often highlighted these differences, leading to debates and discussions within the scientific community.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the field of wireless communication, they were also controversial and not universally accepted at the time. Modern understanding of wireless communication combines elements from both Tesla's and Hertz's theories, acknowledging that electromagnetic waves can propagate through space and that the Earth and atmosphere can act as conductors for certain types of energy transmission.
