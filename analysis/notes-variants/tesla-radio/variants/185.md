Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then known as the Hertzian wave theory, in fully explaining his conception of wireless technology. Tesla believed that the way Hertzian waves were understood and utilized did not align with his vision for wireless communication and energy transmission.

Tesla's work on wireless technology was based on his observations and experiments with high-frequency alternating currents and resonant circuits. He argued that the methods used to transmit and receive signals using Hertzian waves were not sufficient for practical applications of wireless technology as he envisioned them. Specifically, Tesla believed that the use of low-frequency radio waves could be problematic due to their inability to travel long distances without significant loss of power.

In contrast, Tesla proposed that higher frequencies and resonant systems could be more effective for wireless communication and power transmission. He suggested that by using resonant circuits, one could achieve more efficient and longer-range transmission of energy and information. This idea was part of his broader concept of "wireless" or "resonant" energy transmission, which he believed could transmit power over vast distances without the need for physical conductors.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and influential, they were also ahead of their time in many respects. The technology and understanding of electromagnetism at the time were not yet advanced enough to fully validate Tesla's theories. It wasn't until later, with advancements in technology and further research, that some aspects of Tesla's ideas began to be realized and understood.

Today, we recognize that both Tesla's and Hertz's contributions are integral to our understanding of wireless technology. While Tesla's ideas about resonant systems and higher frequencies have been validated to an extent, the practical implementation of these concepts is still a subject of ongoing research and development.
