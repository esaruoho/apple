Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, particularly as it was being developed and applied by others during his time. Tesla's work on radio communication predates many of the developments attributed to Guglielmo Marconi, who is often credited with the invention of radio.

Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves (named after Heinrich Hertz, who demonstrated the existence of electromagnetic waves) was not sufficient for understanding or utilizing wireless communication. He argued that these waves were too weak and did not travel far enough to be practical for long-distance communication. Instead, Tesla proposed that wireless signals could be transmitted through the Earth itself, a concept he referred to as "earth currents" or "resonant circuits."

In his patents and writings, Tesla described methods for transmitting and receiving signals using resonant circuits, which he believed could achieve much greater range and efficiency than the Hertzian wave approach. He conducted experiments that demonstrated the ability to transmit signals over long distances without relying on the air as a medium for propagation.

Tesla's views on the limitations of Hertzian waves were partly based on his experimental observations and theoretical considerations. However, it's important to note that the scientific community at the time, including many of his contemporaries, did not fully accept Tesla's theories and methods. The widespread adoption of radio technology in the early 20th century was largely based on the Hertzian wave theory, although Tesla's contributions were eventually recognized and acknowledged.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's theories have their merits and applications. Modern radio technology incorporates principles from both approaches, allowing for efficient and reliable wireless communication.
