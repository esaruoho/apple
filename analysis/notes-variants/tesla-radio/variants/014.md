Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work on radio waves. Tesla believed that the theoretical framework of Hertzian waves did not fully capture the essence of his own wireless transmission methods and principles.

Tesla's concerns stemmed from several aspects:

Continuous Wave vs. Spark Discharge: Tesla advocated for continuous wave transmission, which involved sending out a steady stream of energy. In contrast, Hertz's experiments used spark discharges, which produced brief pulses of energy. Tesla believed that continuous wave transmission was more efficient and better suited for long-distance communication.

Interference and Signal Quality: Tesla was concerned that the discrete nature of Hertzian waves could lead to interference and signal degradation over long distances. He believed that continuous wave transmission would be less prone to such issues.

Wireless Power Transmission: Tesla's vision extended beyond mere communication. He envisioned the ability to transmit power wirelessly, which he believed could be achieved through continuous wave transmission. This concept was far more ambitious than what Hertzian waves could achieve at the time.

Patent Issues: Tesla also had patent disputes with others who were working on radio technology, which may have influenced his views on the appropriateness of the prevailing theories.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and groundbreaking, they were also ahead of their time in many respects. The technology to fully implement his concepts did not exist during his lifetime. However, his contributions to the field of wireless communication laid the groundwork for much of modern telecommunications.

Despite Tesla's skepticism, the Hertzian wave theory eventually became the foundation for practical radio technology. It was only later, with advancements in technology and further research, that many of Tesla's ideas began to be realized.
