Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then known as the Hertzian wave theory, in fully explaining his conception of wireless technology. Tesla believed that the theory did not accurately describe the phenomena he observed and experienced with his inventions.

Tesla's work on wireless transmission was based on his observations of electrical resonance and the behavior of electrical circuits. He argued that the traditional view of electromagnetic waves as requiring a physical medium for propagation (such as air or a vacuum) was insufficient to explain the long-distance transmission of signals without wires. Tesla's experiments and theories suggested that wireless communication could be achieved through the manipulation of electrical resonances in the Earth's ionosphere and the atmosphere.

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory, which was developed by Heinrich Hertz, described electromagnetic waves as propagating through space as disturbances in an electric and magnetic field. While this theory was groundbreaking and provided a mathematical framework for understanding electromagnetic waves, Tesla felt it did not capture the full potential of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's ideas were ahead of their time and were not widely accepted during his lifetime. However, his contributions to the field of wireless communication laid the groundwork for modern radio and telecommunications technologies. The debate between Tesla's views and the established Hertzian wave theory continued for many years and remains a topic of interest among historians and scientists today.
