Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then known as the Hertzian wave theory. Tesla believed that this theory did not fully capture the essence of wireless communication as he envisioned it. He argued that the concept of Hertzian waves, which were understood as being transmitted through space without wires, did not accurately represent the nature of his own experiments and discoveries.

Tesla's work on wireless transmission was based on the idea of radiant energy and the propagation of electrical currents through the Earth and the ionosphere. He believed that the Earth itself could act as a conductor for these currents, allowing for the transmission of signals over vast distances without the need for physical wires. This concept is often referred to as "earth resonance" or "resonance transmission."

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory primarily focused on the transmission of electromagnetic waves through free space, which Tesla felt was an incomplete description of the phenomenon. He believed that the Earth played a crucial role in the transmission and reception of these signals, and that the waves traveled along the surface of the Earth rather than through it.

Tesla's views on this matter were expressed in various writings and speeches throughout his career. For example, in his 1900 lecture "On Light and Other High Frequency Phenomena," Tesla discussed his theories on wireless transmission and criticized what he saw as the limitations of the Hertzian wave theory. He argued that the theory did not adequately explain the phenomena observed in his experiments, such as the ability to transmit signals over long distances without the use of wires.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and influential, they were also controversial and not universally accepted at the time. The scientific community eventually came to accept the Hertzian wave theory as a valid explanation for wireless communication, although Tesla's contributions to the field remain significant and continue to be studied and appreciated today.
