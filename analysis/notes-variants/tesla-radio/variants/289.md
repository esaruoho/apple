Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism about the electromagnetic radiation theory as it was understood during his time. He believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, which were named after Heinrich Hertz who demonstrated the existence of electromagnetic waves, did not fully capture the nature of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission involved a more direct and efficient method than what was being proposed by the prevailing theories of the time. He argued that the transmission of information through the air could be achieved without the need for a physical medium like the waves described by Hertz. Instead, Tesla proposed that wireless communication could be achieved through a form of energy that could penetrate matter, effectively bypassing the need for a conductor or a physical medium to transmit signals.

In his writings and lectures, Tesla often criticized the Hertzian wave theory, suggesting that it was an oversimplification of the complex phenomena involved in wireless communication. He believed that the true nature of wireless transmission was more akin to what he called "radiant energy," which could travel through space without the need for a wave-like propagation mechanism.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and ahead of their time, they were not universally accepted at the time. The scientific community generally accepted the Hertzian wave theory as the correct explanation for electromagnetic radiation and wireless communication. However, Tesla's contributions to the field of wireless technology, including his work on the Wardenclyffe Tower project, have been recognized as significant milestones in the development of modern telecommunications.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's theories play important roles in our understanding of electromagnetic radiation and wireless communication. Tesla's work laid the groundwork for many of the principles used in modern wireless technology, even if his specific theories about the nature of wireless transmission were not entirely accurate according to contemporary scientific understanding.
