Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism about the electromagnetic radiation theory as it was understood during his time, particularly regarding its application to wireless communication. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves (named after Heinrich Hertz, who demonstrated the existence of electromagnetic waves) did not fully capture the nature of wireless transmission as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission could be achieved through a medium that was more akin to a conductor than a simple radiating field. He proposed that wireless signals could travel along the Earth's surface or through the ionosphere, which he referred to as "earth currents" and "space currents," respectively. This idea contrasted with the prevailing view that radio waves traveled through space without a medium.

In his writings and lectures, Tesla argued that the Hertzian wave theory was incomplete and that it failed to account for the phenomena he observed and utilized in his experiments. For example, Tesla claimed that his systems could transmit signals over much greater distances than those predicted by the Hertzian wave theory, and he attributed this to the use of ground waves and sky waves, which were not fully explained by the existing theory.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the development of wireless technology, they also led to some controversy and misunderstanding. The scientific community at the time largely accepted the Hertzian wave theory as the basis for radio communication, and it was only later that many of Tesla's concepts were validated and incorporated into modern theories of electromagnetic propagation.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's theories play roles in different aspects of wireless communication. Tesla's contributions to the understanding of wireless transmission have been recognized, and his work continues to inspire researchers and engineers in the field of wireless technology.
