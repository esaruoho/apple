Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work on radio waves. Tesla believed that the theoretical framework developed by Hertz and others did not fully capture the essence of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his understanding of the nature of electricity and magnetism, which he saw as more than just the propagation of waves through space. He emphasized the importance of what he called "resonance" or "sympathetic vibration," suggesting that the key to effective wireless transmission lay in the resonance between the transmitter and receiver rather than simply the transmission of electromagnetic waves.

In his view, the concept of electromagnetic waves as discrete particles traveling through space could be misleading. Tesla argued that the true mechanism of wireless communication involved a form of energy transfer that was more akin to the transfer of electrical energy through conductors, but extended over vast distances without wires.

This perspective led Tesla to develop and advocate for systems that relied on resonant circuits and other forms of energy transfer, which he believed would be more efficient and effective for long-distance communication. His ideas were often at odds with the prevailing scientific consensus of his time, which was heavily influenced by the work of Hertz and Maxwell.

It's important to note that while Tesla's theories were innovative and influential, they were also controversial and not universally accepted during his lifetime. The development of practical radio technology ultimately relied more on the principles established by Hertz and later refined by others, including Guglielmo Marconi, who successfully demonstrated wireless telegraphy using Hertzian waves.

Despite these differences, Tesla's contributions to the field of wireless communication remain significant, and his ideas continue to inspire researchers and engineers working on advanced forms of wireless technology today.
