Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism about the electromagnetic radiation theory as it was understood during his time, particularly regarding its application to wireless communication. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, which were named after Heinrich Hertz who demonstrated the existence of electromagnetic waves, did not fully capture the nature of wireless transmission as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission could be achieved through a medium that was more akin to a conductor than a simple wave propagating through space. He proposed that the Earth itself could act as a conductor and that wireless signals could travel along the surface of the Earth or through the ionosphere, rather than simply through the air as described by the Hertzian wave theory.

In his writings and lectures, Tesla often criticized what he saw as the limitations of the Hertzian wave theory, arguing that it did not account for the phenomena he observed and experienced in his experiments with wireless transmission. For example, Tesla claimed that his systems could transmit signals over much greater distances and with less power than those based on the Hertzian wave theory alone.

It's important to note that while Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the field of wireless communication, they also led to some controversy and misunderstanding. The scientific community at the time generally accepted the Hertzian wave theory as the basis for wireless communication, although it is now recognized that both theories have their merits and can coexist in explaining different aspects of wireless technology.

Today, we understand that wireless communication involves a combination of both ground wave propagation and electromagnetic radiation, and Tesla's contributions to this field are widely acknowledged, even if his specific theories about the nature of wireless transmission were not entirely accurate according to modern scientific understanding.
