Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, particularly as it was being developed and applied by others during his time. Tesla's work on radio communication predates many of the developments attributed to Guglielmo Marconi, who is often credited with the invention of radio.

Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves (electromagnetic waves as described by Heinrich Hertz) did not fully capture the nature of wireless communication as he understood it. He argued that the transmission of signals through the air was not simply a matter of sending waves that could be detected at a distance, but rather involved a more complex interaction between the transmitter and receiver.

Some key points of Tesla's critique include:

Resonance: Tesla emphasized the importance of resonance in wireless communication. He believed that the Earth itself could act as a resonant medium for transmitting signals, which would allow for much longer-range communication than what was possible with simple Hertzian waves.

Coherence: Tesla argued that the signals should be coherent, meaning they should maintain their integrity over long distances without dissipating or losing strength. This was in contrast to the idea that signals would spread out and weaken as they traveled through space.

Interference: Tesla was concerned about the interference that could occur with Hertzian waves, especially in crowded frequency bands. He believed that a better approach would involve using specific frequencies that could be tuned into by receivers, similar to how musical instruments resonate at certain frequencies.

Patents and Prior Art: Tesla also disputed the claims of inventors like Marconi, arguing that his own patents and prior work were foundational to the development of radio technology. He felt that his earlier inventions and patents had been overlooked or ignored.

Despite these differences, it's important to note that Tesla's contributions to the field of wireless communication were significant, even if his ideas about the nature of electromagnetic radiation were not universally accepted at the time. His work laid the groundwork for modern radio technology, and his theories continue to be studied and debated in the context of wireless communication and electromagnetic theory.
