Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism and criticism towards the electromagnetic radiation theory as it was understood during his time. Tesla's views were rooted in his own experimental work and theoretical framework, which he believed provided a more accurate description of wireless communication.

Tesla's primary concerns with the electromagnetic radiation theory, as proposed by Heinrich Hertz, were related to the nature of the waves and their interaction with matter. He argued that the concept of "Hertzian waves" did not fully capture the essence of wireless transmission as he envisioned it. Tesla believed that the energy transmitted through space was not just a wave but also involved a form of radiant energy that could be directly influenced by the transmitter and receiver.

In his writings and lectures, Tesla often emphasized the importance of the "ether" or "medium" through which the energy traveled. He suggested that this medium was not simply a passive carrier of waves but an active participant in the transmission process. This view contrasted with the prevailing interpretation of Hertzian waves, which treated them as purely mechanical disturbances propagating through a vacuum.

Furthermore, Tesla's experiments with resonant circuits and his development of the Wardenclyffe Tower project were based on his belief in the existence of a global electrical circuit that could facilitate long-distance wireless communication without the need for physical conductors. He saw the Earth itself as a conductor and believed that the proper tuning of transmitters and receivers could allow for efficient and long-range communication.

Despite these differences, it is important to note that Tesla's contributions to the field of wireless communication were significant, and many of his ideas have been validated over time. The modern understanding of electromagnetic radiation and wireless communication incorporates elements from both Tesla's and Hertz's theories, reflecting the complex interplay between scientific discovery and innovation.
