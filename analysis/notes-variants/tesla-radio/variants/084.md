Nikola Tesla indeed expressed skepticism about the electromagnetic radiation theory as it was understood during his time. He believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, which were named after Heinrich Hertz who demonstrated the existence of electromagnetic waves, did not fully capture the essence of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission involved a more direct and efficient method than what was being proposed by the prevailing scientific community at the time. He argued that the idea of transmitting signals through the air as waves was too indirect and inefficient for practical applications. Instead, Tesla advocated for a system where energy could be transmitted directly from one point to another without the need for a physical medium like air or space.

One of the key points of contention between Tesla and the proponents of Hertzian wave theory was the nature of the signal's propagation. Tesla believed that signals could be transmitted through the Earth itself, a concept he referred to as "earth currents" or "terrestrial resonance." This idea was based on his experiments with resonant circuits and his belief that the Earth could act as a conductor for these signals.

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory focused on the idea of electromagnetic waves propagating through the air, which was seen as a more straightforward explanation of how radio waves could travel from one location to another. However, this approach required a much larger antenna and was less efficient for long-distance communication.

Despite Tesla's reservations, the Hertzian wave theory eventually became the accepted model for understanding radio waves. It provided a framework that could explain the observed phenomena and was more amenable to practical applications in radio communication. Nonetheless, Tesla's ideas and experiments laid the groundwork for many modern technologies, including wireless power transmission and resonant circuitry, which continue to be areas of active research and development today.
