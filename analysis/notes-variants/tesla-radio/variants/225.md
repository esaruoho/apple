Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work on radio waves. Tesla believed that the theoretical framework developed by Hertz and others did not fully capture the essence of his own wireless transmission methods.

Tesla's approach to wireless communication was based on a different set of principles compared to the prevailing scientific understanding of his time. He emphasized the use of resonant circuits and the concept of "earth as a conductor" for transmitting signals over long distances without the need for physical antennas or towers. This approach was fundamentally different from the idea of propagating radio waves through space.

In his writings and public demonstrations, Tesla argued that the Hertzian wave theory, which described radio waves as traveling through space, was an incomplete model for understanding wireless communication. He believed that the theory did not account for the resonant properties of the Earth and the atmosphere, which he thought were crucial for efficient wireless transmission.

Tesla's claims were controversial at the time and continue to be debated among historians and scientists. While modern science has largely validated the Hertzian wave theory as a fundamental part of our understanding of electromagnetic radiation, it is also true that Tesla's contributions to wireless technology have been recognized and celebrated. His work laid the groundwork for many of the technologies we use today, including radio, television, and radar.

It's important to note that Tesla's views on the limitations of the Hertzian wave theory were part of a broader debate in the early 20th century about the nature of electromagnetic phenomena. The scientific community eventually came to accept both Tesla's and Hertz's theories as complementary parts of our understanding of electromagnetism.
