Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, which was primarily based on Heinrich Hertz's work. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, or radio waves as they are commonly known today, did not fully capture the essence of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission could be achieved through a medium that was more than just the propagation of electromagnetic waves through space. He proposed that there was a "wireless" medium that could carry signals without the need for physical conductors. This idea was encapsulated in his concept of "resonance," where the Earth and the ionosphere could act as a resonant system to transmit and receive signals.

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory focused on the propagation of electromagnetic waves through free space, which was seen as a more direct and measurable phenomenon. Tesla argued that this approach was limited and did not fully explain the phenomena he observed and utilized in his experiments.

One of the key points of contention was the nature of the signal's propagation. Tesla believed that signals could be transmitted through the Earth itself, using the Earth as a conductor, rather than relying solely on the air as a medium for radio waves. This idea was ahead of its time and was not widely accepted during Tesla's lifetime. However, modern research has shown that Tesla's concept of using the Earth as a conductor for wireless communication is valid and forms the basis for technologies such as groundwave propagation and long-distance radio transmission.

Despite these differences, both Tesla's and Hertz's theories have contributed significantly to our understanding of wireless communication. Today, we use a combination of both approaches to achieve efficient and reliable wireless communication systems.
