Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work on radio waves. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves (or electromagnetic waves) did not fully capture the essence of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's views were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission could be achieved through a medium that was more akin to what he called "radiant energy," which encompassed both electric and magnetic fields but also included other forms of energy that were not yet understood or recognized in the scientific community at the time. He argued that the traditional view of electromagnetic waves as propagating through space in a vacuum was incomplete and did not account for the full potential of wireless communication.

In particular, Tesla contended that the idea of sending signals through the air as waves could not fully explain phenomena such as the ability to transmit signals over long distances without direct line-of-sight, or the way that signals could be received even when the transmitter and receiver were not in close proximity. He believed that there was a deeper, more fundamental principle at work that involved a form of energy that could permeate the Earth and the atmosphere in a manner that allowed for efficient and reliable wireless communication.

While Tesla's ideas were groundbreaking and contributed significantly to the development of modern wireless technology, they were also controversial and not widely accepted during his lifetime. It wasn't until later that many of his concepts were validated and integrated into the broader understanding of electromagnetic theory and wireless communication.

It's important to note that Tesla's contributions to the field of wireless communication are well-documented and include the development of the Tesla coil, which is still used in various applications today, including radio frequency heating and electronic music instruments. His work laid the foundation for much of the technology we use in modern telecommunications.
