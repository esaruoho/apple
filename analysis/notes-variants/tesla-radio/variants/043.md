Nikola Tesla indeed made claims about the limitations of the electromagnetic radiation theory, which was then primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz's work on radio waves. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, or what we now call radio waves, did not fully capture his vision of wireless communication and energy transmission.

Tesla's ideas were rooted in his belief that wireless transmission could be achieved through a medium that allowed for more efficient and widespread distribution of electrical energy without the need for physical antennas or conductors. He proposed that the Earth itself could act as a conductor, allowing for the transmission of electrical signals over vast distances without the need for the high-frequency oscillations that were characteristic of Hertzian waves.

In contrast, Tesla advocated for low-frequency currents, which he believed could penetrate the Earth and propagate without the need for direct line-of-sight transmission. This approach was based on his experiments with resonant circuits and his belief in the existence of a "worldwide web" of electrical energy, which he termed "wireless transmission."

While Tesla's ideas were groundbreaking and have influenced modern technology, particularly in the realm of wireless communication, they were also controversial at the time. Many scientists and engineers of the late 19th and early 20th centuries were skeptical of Tesla's claims and preferred the more established theories of electromagnetic radiation.

It's important to note that Tesla's work laid the groundwork for much of the modern wireless technology we use today, including radio, television, and mobile communications. However, his specific theories about the nature of wireless transmission were not widely accepted during his lifetime and are still subjects of debate among historians and scientists.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's contributions are integral to our current understanding of electromagnetic waves and wireless communication. While Tesla's ideas were innovative and ahead of their time, they did not fully replace the established theories but rather complemented them, leading to the development of modern wireless technologies.
