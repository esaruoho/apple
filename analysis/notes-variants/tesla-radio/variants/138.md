Nikola Tesla indeed had significant disagreements with the prevailing electromagnetic radiation theory, which was primarily associated with Heinrich Hertz. Tesla believed that the concept of Hertzian waves, or electromagnetic waves propagating through space, did not fully capture the essence of wireless communication as he envisioned it.

Tesla's conception of wireless transmission was rooted in his belief that the Earth itself could be used as a conductor for transmitting information and energy over vast distances. He proposed that wireless signals could be sent through the ground rather than through the air, which he thought would be more efficient and less susceptible to interference.

In contrast, the Hertzian wave theory, which was gaining acceptance at the time, suggested that electromagnetic waves could propagate through free space without requiring a physical medium. This theory was based on experimental evidence showing that electromagnetic waves could travel through a vacuum, similar to light.

Tesla argued that the Hertzian wave theory was incomplete and potentially misleading because it did not account for the role of the Earth as a conductor in wireless communication. He believed that the Earth's ionosphere and the ground itself played crucial roles in the transmission and reception of wireless signals.

This disagreement between Tesla and the Hertzian wave theory is part of the broader debate in the early 20th century about the nature of wireless communication. While Tesla's ideas were innovative and contributed significantly to the development of wireless technology, they were not widely accepted during his lifetime. It wasn't until later that advancements in technology and further research confirmed aspects of Tesla's theories, particularly in the context of ground wave propagation and long-distance radio communication.

Today, we understand that both Tesla's and Hertz's contributions are important. Tesla's work laid the groundwork for understanding the practical applications of wireless communication, while Hertz's experiments provided the theoretical foundation for the science behind it.
